Front matter · Executive summary

Executive summary

De-extinction is often discussed as a question of biological capability: whether genetic engineering, cloning, selective breeding, and reproductive technologies can recreate organisms resembling extinct species. This report asks a different question. Even where a technically viable proxy can be produced, is its creation and release ethically defensible when the intervention may consume scarce resources, create new animal-welfare burdens, alter ecosystems, and affect communities whose lands and livelihoods are implicated?

This report develops a critical, human-welfare-centred assessment of de-extinction. It considers genetic reconstruction, back-breeding, cloning and other reproductive pathways; the uncertain identity of reconstructed organisms; the welfare of embryos, surrogate animals and offspring; ecological risks after release; opportunity costs; the distribution of economic gains and burdens; Indigenous sovereignty; and the moral status of current humanitarian needs. The conclusion is normative: scientific possibility does not by itself establish moral permission. The report argues that any proposal must be assessed against duties to living people, animals used in research, future generations, and communities with rights and interests in affected territories.

Principal findings and propositions

  1. Feasibility is not equivalence. A genetically edited or selectively bred proxy may resemble an extinct organism without recreating the original genome, developmental history, learned behaviour, microbiome, or ecological relationships. "De-extinction" can therefore describe a family of different interventions rather than a single biological achievement.[1][2][3]
  2. Animal welfare belongs in the feasibility assessment. Reproductive procedures can impose risks on donor animals, surrogate mothers, embryos, and offspring. The welfare profile must be evaluated per method and species; historic somatic-cell nuclear-transfer outcomes cannot be treated as a universal success rate for all contemporary pathways.[10][11]
  3. Ecological benefit is contingent, not automatic. Reintroduction outcomes depend on habitat suitability, ecological interactions, disease, social acceptance, governance, and long-term funding. Conservation translocation guidelines already emphasize risk assessment, feasibility, monitoring, and stakeholder engagement.[4][5]
  4. Opportunity cost is the strongest economic question. A de-extinction budget is not necessarily money removed dollar-for-dollar from poverty programmes. However, public finance, skilled labour, land, research infrastructure, and political attention are finite or rivalrous resources. The report therefore models displacement as a parameter, not as an assumption that every private dollar would otherwise fund public services.
  5. Distribution matters as much as aggregate spending. Wealthy firms and research institutions may capture intellectual-property value, while nearby communities could bear monitoring costs, land-use restrictions, conflict risks, or changes in access. The distribution is project-specific and must be assessed rather than presumed.
  6. Indigenous consent is a substantive ethical constraint. Indigenous peoples and local communities are not passive "stakeholders" in territories they govern or depend upon. International standards recognize rights to participation, consultation, and free, prior and informed consent in relevant contexts; the report argues for an explicit right to refuse projects affecting ancestral territories, while distinguishing that normative recommendation from the variable domestic legal enforceability of such a veto.[40][41]
  7. Humanitarian reinvestment can improve welfare, and can be described as a cure. Reallocating resources could support health services, education, food systems, housing, clean water, and climate adaptation. Outcomes depend on implementation, institutions, distribution, absorptive capacity, and conflict conditions.
Core ethical position. Under the report's egalitarian framework, human survival, basic welfare, and equal dignity take priority over speculative resurrection projects. This priority does not require treating animal suffering as irrelevant: it requires that research-related suffering and ecological risks be justified by benefits that are sufficiently credible, proportionate, and fairly distributed.
Front matter · Purpose and position

Research question, thesis, and analytical boundaries

The core question is whether the development and deployment of de-extinction technologies can be justified when evaluated against competing obligations to living populations, ecological systems, research animals, and communities whose territories may be affected.

Central thesis supplied by the project author

Thesis: De-extinction is fundamentally immoral. It will harm humanity, and it will destroy tribal lands and undermine equality that we have among people. Ruling de-extinction is unethical would stop humanity from doing that, would solve poverty, would solve inequality, would solve hunger, would solve inadequate housing, and would solve a whole host of problems that humanity currently possesses.

The report uses this statement as the project's normative thesis. Its empirical sections test the causal premises that connect de-extinction investment to human welfare, inequality, land governance, and environmental outcomes.

Scope of the term "de-extinction"

"De-extinction" is used broadly here to cover (i) genetic engineering of living relatives to create organisms with selected extinct traits, (ii) selective breeding or back-breeding toward an extinct phenotype, and (iii) cloning or other reproductive techniques intended to recreate organisms from preserved biological material. These approaches differ biologically and ethically. A report that treats them as a single technology risks attributing the limitations of one method to all others.

Part I · Definitions and scope

1. De-extinction as a family of interventions

De-extinction is not a single procedure with a single endpoint. It is an umbrella term used for technical approaches that aim to recover some combination of the genetic, morphological, behavioural, ecological, or evolutionary characteristics of an extinct taxon. A genetic proxy can carry selected alleles from an extinct genome while developing within the reproductive and ecological context of a living relative. A back-bred population can resemble an extinct phenotype while remaining genetically distinct. A cloned individual, where viable nuclei are available, may share a closer nuclear-genome relationship with a source organism but still develop through an egg cell, gestational environment, microbiome, and social system that differ from the extinct lineage.

These distinctions matter for ethics. A project might be justified as a way to create a functional ecological analogue, as a form of species repair, as a scientific experiment, as a commercial attraction, or as a symbolic act of restitution. Each justification carries different standards of evidence. The ecological rationale asks whether the proxy can contribute to a specified ecosystem function without disproportionate harm. The species-repair rationale asks what "restoration" means when the original population, its learned behaviours, and its historical relationships have disappeared. The scientific rationale must explain why the same knowledge cannot be gained through less harmful methods. Commercial and symbolic rationales cannot be treated as conservation benefits unless outcomes are measured against stated targets.

For the report's purposes, the central unit of analysis is not only the individual animal. It is the programme: the full sequence of sample collection, genome analysis, editing, reproductive trials, animal care, population establishment, transport, release, monitoring, governance, and eventual contingency response. Cost and harm estimates that end when the first live animal is born understate the likely resource and welfare burden of projects intended to establish free-living populations.

Operational definition. A de-extinction programme is treated as a coordinated research and management intervention that seeks to generate, reproduce, or release an organism intended to recover selected traits or functions associated with an extinct taxon. Its lifecycle includes failed as well as successful attempts, pre-release and post-release management, and obligations to affected communities.
Part II · Scientific foundations

2. Scientific pathways and technical uncertainty

2.1 Genome reconstruction and editing

For many extinct organisms, no intact viable cell line exists. Researchers therefore rely on preserved fragments, museum specimens, subfossil remains, or other degraded biological material. Sequencing technologies can recover substantial portions of ancient DNA, but gaps, contamination, chemical damage, repeated regions, and uncertainty about structural variation complicate genome reconstruction. A complete sequence reconstructed computationally is not equivalent to an intact living nucleus. Nor does a reference genome necessarily capture the within-population variation required for long-term population health.

Genome editing can alter a living relative at selected sites, potentially introducing traits associated with the extinct taxon. Yet complex traits rarely map neatly to a short list of independent switches. Regulatory sequences, epigenetic states, developmental timing, maternal effects, microbiota, diet, social learning, and environmental conditions all contribute to phenotype. The more ambitious the objective—such as recreating cold tolerance, body size, digestive physiology, or a specialised behaviour—the more difficult it becomes to predict how edited traits will interact. A proxy may be a valuable research organism without constituting the return of an extinct species.

2.2 Reproductive technologies

Reproductive pathways vary with the organism and the availability of compatible living relatives. Nuclear transfer requires viable or recoverable donor nuclei, compatible oocytes, successful reprogramming, embryo development, and gestation in a surrogate. Genome-edited embryos may use assisted reproductive techniques but still require suitable gametes, gestational carriers, or artificial development systems. Each pathway has distinct failure modes. It is methodologically inappropriate to apply a single "success rate" to all projects or species.

Programme planning must count attempts rather than celebrate only the surviving offspring. A technically successful birth may follow many failed embryos, unsuccessful implantations, pregnancy losses, neonatal deaths, or animals that survive but experience poor welfare. The ethical denominator is therefore the total number of animals and reproductive events involved, not merely the number of living proxies produced. Where published studies report success rates, they must be interpreted within the specific species, protocol, laboratory, and definition of success used by the study.

2.3 Population viability

Producing one or several organisms is not equivalent to recovering a self-sustaining population. A viable population may require sufficient effective population size, genetic diversity, reproductive compatibility, disease resilience, demographic balance, suitable habitat, and a credible plan for environmental change. The founders may be genetically narrow, related, or dependent on repeated assisted reproduction. Population managers may need to control breeding, translocate individuals, supplement genetic variation, or maintain semi-captive conditions. These interventions change the meaning of "wild restoration" and add recurrent costs.

2.4 From laboratory achievement to conservation outcome

Scientific novelty, public attention, and conservation effectiveness are separate outcome dimensions. A project may produce a technically interesting organism yet fail to establish a stable population. It may establish a population but fail to improve the target ecological function. It may produce a measurable function while imposing unacceptable costs on other animals or local communities. This report therefore treats each stage as a decision gate requiring evidence, not as an inevitable progression from "successful" research to "successful" conservation.

Decision principle. Before proceeding to release, proponents should demonstrate a clear conservation objective, adequate habitat, a defensible risk assessment, an animal-welfare plan, long-term financing, and consent from affected rights-holders. The more uncertain the ecological and social consequences, the stronger the case for staged and reversible intervention.
Part II · Taxonomy and identity

3. Can a reconstructed organism be the same extinct species?

The term "same species" can mean several things. It may refer to genetic similarity, membership in an evolutionary lineage, reproductive compatibility, shared ecological function, or a taxonomic designation. These criteria do not always converge. A proxy engineered from a living relative may contain some extinct-associated alleles but remain nested within the living species' developmental lineage. A back-bred animal may show a recognisable phenotype without recovering the extinct species' unique genome. A cloned organism, even if derived from an extinct nucleus, would develop under contemporary maternal, microbial, social, and environmental conditions.

Species concepts are themselves plural. Biological species concepts emphasise reproductive isolation; phylogenetic concepts emphasise diagnosable evolutionary lineages; ecological concepts emphasise niches and roles. An organism could resemble an extinct species morphologically yet fail to reproduce with it (which is impossible to test when no original individuals remain), or it could perform a comparable function while belonging to a different lineage. A claim that a species has been "brought back" can therefore be stronger than the biological evidence warrants.

3.1 The proxy problem

Calling a reconstructed animal a proxy is often more precise than calling it a resurrected member of the extinct species. This language does not diminish the possible scientific value of the organism. It does, however, clarify the conservation claim. If the goal is to restore a grazing function, a seed-dispersal interaction, or a predator-prey relationship, evidence should focus on that function. If the goal is to restore an extinct lineage, genomic and evolutionary continuity becomes more central. If the goal is symbolic restitution, the moral rationale must confront the fact that biological resemblance does not reverse the historical loss of the original population.

3.2 Why species identity changes the ethical calculus

Species identity affects how success is measured, how conservation status is described, whether ecological risk can be justified, and whether financial or legal commitments are framed as species recovery. It can also influence public support. A project promoted as the return of a lost species may be received differently from a project openly described as the creation of a novel organism inspired by an extinct genome. Ethical legitimacy depends partly on transparent communication about that distinction.

Report position. A reconstructed proxy should not automatically inherit the ecological, taxonomic, or moral status of the extinct species it resembles. Claims of restoration must specify which dimensions—genetic, taxonomic, functional, or symbolic—are actually being pursued and demonstrated.
Part III · Animal welfare

4. Reproductive attrition, surrogate burden, and animal welfare

Animal welfare is not a secondary question to be considered after technical feasibility. It is part of the cost of producing a proxy. Reproductive interventions can involve ovarian stimulation, egg retrieval, embryo manipulation, implantation, gestation, birth, neonatal care, and repeated attempts following failure. A surrogate may be exposed to risks associated with pregnancy and delivery, while donor animals may undergo procedures that are painful, stressful, or medically complex. Embryos and offspring may experience developmental abnormalities, compromised health, or difficult adaptation to captive or wild environments.

The best-known cloning literature documents significant technical and welfare concerns in somatic cell nuclear transfer. Reviews describe inefficient reprogramming and a range of developmental or placental abnormalities in some mammalian cloning applications. The reported efficiency figures are species- and protocol-specific. They should not be converted into one universal rate for all future de-extinction methods. The ethical implication is still clear: programme proposals must report the denominator of embryos, pregnancies, surrogate animals, live births, and surviving healthy animals, with a transparent account of adverse outcomes.

4.2 The moral status of surrogate animals

Surrogate animals are not merely biological equipment. They are sentient individuals with welfare interests of their own. Their use requires a justification that considers the probability and magnitude of harm, the availability of alternatives, the likely benefit, and whether benefits accrue to the animals or ecosystems affected. Even where an extinct organism's return might produce ecological benefits, those benefits do not automatically justify repeated reproductive harm to living animals. A precautionary approach requires welfare limits, independent oversight, transparent stopping rules, and an assessment of whether a less harmful method could meet the same objective.

4.3 A precautionary stopping rule

Research protocols should specify in advance how many unsuccessful attempts, maternal complications, abnormal offspring, or welfare incidents trigger suspension or termination. Continuing a project because significant money has already been spent is a form of sunk-cost reasoning, not an ethical justification. The burden of proof belongs to the programme proponents: they should demonstrate a credible benefit and acceptable welfare profile before escalating the number of animals exposed.

Ethical threshold. A project should not be considered successful solely because a live proxy is born. A defensible success metric must include animal health, quality of life, social functioning, reproductive viability, ecological role, and the total welfare burden required to obtain those outcomes.
Part IV · Ecosystem and reintroduction risk

5. Ecological uncertainty and the management burden after release

Extinction is not only the absence of an individual organism. It is the loss of populations, evolutionary histories, mutualisms, predator-prey relationships, disease dynamics, and the ecological context in which the species developed. Ecosystems continue to change after a species disappears. Climate, land use, prey density, vegetation, pathogens, competitors, and human settlement patterns can shift over decades or centuries. An organism that once occupied a habitat may no longer be suited to the present-day conditions of that habitat.

Ecological restoration can improve biodiversity and ecosystem services, but that evidence does not mean every reintroduction will be beneficial. Meta-analyses of restoration interventions show that restoration can improve biodiversity and service provision on average; the magnitude and durability of outcomes vary by ecosystem, intervention, and monitoring period.[22] Reintroduction guidelines accordingly call for clear objectives, assessment of causes of original decline, habitat suitability, disease risk, stakeholder engagement, and post-release monitoring.[4][5]

5.1 Reintroduction is not a neutral act

Large animals can alter vegetation, prey populations, water access, livestock practices, and land use. These effects can be positive for some ecological processes and harmful to some households or economic activities. Human-wildlife conflict research documents crop loss, livestock depredation, injury, fear, retaliatory killing, and political disputes, while also describing coexistence measures and compensation systems. The scale and distribution of impacts depend on the species, landscape, governance, and local economy; it is not defensible to presume either universal harm or universal ecological benefit.

The relevant lesson for de-extinction is not that every large animal will trigger conflict. It is that ecological restoration takes place in lived landscapes, not empty spaces. Reintroduction plans must address land tenure, seasonal use, subsistence activities, public safety, livestock, food security, local knowledge, and who pays for prevention and compensation. A project that externalises these costs onto nearby communities is ethically incomplete even if the ecological objective is sincere.

5.2 Path dependence and irreversible effects

Once a population is released, management options may become more difficult. Animals may spread beyond planned boundaries, establish breeding populations, transmit pathogens, compete with existing species, or create conflicts that require culling or containment. These outcomes are not inevitable, but the possibility of control failure should be part of the risk analysis. A programme should identify a credible exit strategy, including the conditions under which animals may be recaptured, relocated, sterilised, or otherwise managed without imposing unacceptable suffering.

5.3 Reintroduction conflict: real evidence and hypothetical case vignettes

Published wildlife-reintroduction and human-wildlife conflict studies provide real evidence about the kinds of disputes that can arise around predators, herbivores, livestock, crop damage, compensation, and land management. This report does not invent historical events.

Part V · Economic framework

6. The economics of large-scale de-extinction

Cost estimates for de-extinction are unusually sensitive to programme definition. A proof-of-concept experiment, a captive breeding programme, a multi-species portfolio, and a globally distributed network of self-sustaining populations are not comparable projects. A serious estimate must include research and development, biological sample acquisition, genome sequencing, computational analysis, editing reagents, embryo creation, surrogate care, veterinary treatment, failed attempts, facilities, habitat acquisition or restoration, transportation, monitoring, community agreements, compensation, and long-term contingency reserves.

6.2 Three programme scales

The report distinguishes three potential scopes because ethical and economic claims change with scale. Attempting to reconstruct every known extinct species is a radically different project from supporting a limited number of carefully chosen proxies. Establishing viable free-living populations worldwide is more demanding still, because it requires long-term habitat suitability, governance, and monitoring across many jurisdictions.

6.3 Public and private finance are not interchangeable

Private investment does not automatically become a public opportunity cost. A private biotech company may fund research from its own capital, and the counterfactual use of that capital may be another commercial venture rather than a food programme or a health clinic. Yet private projects can still use scarce researchers, public research infrastructure, public grants, land, tax incentives, public regulatory capacity, or publicly funded conservation partners. Public money may also be used to subsidise infrastructure or absorb liability. For this reason, the report separates (a) public fiscal displacement, (b) private capital allocation, (c) skilled-labour and scientific-capacity displacement, and (d) land and ecosystem opportunity costs.

The economic question is not simply "How much money is spent?" It is "Which resources are scarce, what are their next-best uses, who controls the allocation, and who bears the risk if the programme fails?" This approach prevents an overstatement that every dollar spent on de-extinction would otherwise have been directed to the poor. It also prevents the opposite mistake of assuming that private funding has no social cost.

6.4 Cost uncertainty and the burden of proof

Where direct cost data do not exist, transparent intervals are more informative than a false point estimate. A robust estimate should state the price year, currency, discount rate, programme duration, number of species, number of reproductive attempts, expected population size, land requirements, monitoring obligations, and which costs are included. Costs may fall as tools improve, but they may also rise as programmes move from a laboratory demonstration to multiple generations and free-living populations. The relationship between scale and cost is nonlinear: a small increase in the number of target species could require new facilities, more specialist staff, multiple surrogate species, and additional land-management agreements.

Part V · Opportunity cost

7. The economic and environmental opportunity cost

Opportunity cost is the value of the best feasible alternative forgone when resources are committed to a particular use. In this context, the concept is more precise than saying that every unit of de-extinction spending directly removes an equivalent unit of funding from poverty reduction. A private research grant may not have been available for public housing. But a public budget allocation, a trained reproductive biologist, a university laboratory, a suitable habitat parcel, and policy attention can each have competing uses. Some are transferable; others are only partially fungible. A credible assessment should identify the specific scarce input and its plausible alternative use.

Opportunity costs operate through four channels. First, fiscal displacement: public grants, procurement, tax incentives, or conservation budgets may be allocated to one programme instead of another. Second, human-capital displacement: highly trained scientists, veterinarians, ecological managers, and public administrators have finite time. Third, land and ecological displacement: suitable habitat, water, and access corridors may be allocated to one project rather than local livelihoods, existing conservation, or restoration. Fourth, political-attention displacement: a high-profile technological programme may attract prestige and agenda space that could otherwise support less visible but urgent public needs.

Passage 1 — The Economic and Environmental Opportunity Cost of De-Extinction

The cumulative cost of large-scale de-extinction could reach several trillion dollars when accounting for the extensive research, genetic engineering, reproductive procedures, habitat restoration, and long-term ecological management required to resurrect and sustain extinct species. These expenditures would not end with the initial creation of a resurrected organism. Maintaining viable populations would require continued scientific oversight, specialized facilities, veterinary care, habitat protection, ecological monitoring, and potentially decades of additional investment. The financial burden would therefore extend far beyond the initial research and development phase.

Funding these initiatives would require substantial investments of public and private resources that could otherwise be allocated toward addressing existing global crises, including poverty alleviation, hunger, climate change, and environmental conservation. Governments, universities, research institutions, and private investors all operate with finite resources, and allocating substantial amounts toward de-extinction necessarily creates competing priorities. Even when funding originates from private organizations, the scientific personnel, infrastructure, land, and institutional capacity required for these projects remain resources that could potentially support other humanitarian and environmental objectives.

De-extinction could also divert highly specialized scientific personnel and institutional resources away from urgent socioeconomic and environmental challenges. Researchers working in genetics, reproductive biology, ecological restoration, and biotechnology represent a limited pool of expertise. Redirecting a substantial portion of that expertise toward resurrecting extinct organisms could reduce the capacity available for projects addressing existing biodiversity loss, food insecurity, climate adaptation, and environmental degradation. The consequences would be particularly concerning if de-extinction projects received disproportionate attention because of their commercial appeal, scientific prestige, or potential for private profit.

Rather than improving global ecological conditions, prioritizing de-extinction could leave existing environmental problems underfunded. Resources directed toward resurrecting extinct organisms could instead support renewable energy infrastructure, reforestation, habitat preservation, sustainable agriculture, clean-water access, and climate-resilience programs. These investments address environmental threats that are already affecting living populations and ecosystems. Failing to adequately fund such interventions could increase ecological degradation and leave communities more vulnerable to climate-related disasters.

The opportunity cost is therefore not merely financial. It concerns the humanitarian and environmental benefits that society may lose by prioritizing speculative biological reconstruction over urgent, existing needs. If large-scale de-extinction requires resources that could otherwise reduce emissions, protect vulnerable ecosystems, or improve human living conditions, its implementation could indirectly undermine efforts to combat climate change. The extent of that effect would depend on the scale of investment and the resources displaced, but it represents a significant concern when evaluating the ethical consequences of the technology.

Ultimately, de-extinction could make existing environmental and socioeconomic problems more difficult to address if its financial and institutional demands displace higher-priority interventions. The money, scientific expertise, and infrastructure required for these projects could be directed toward fighting climate change, alleviating poverty, reducing hunger, and improving the conditions in which humanity lives. From this perspective, the ethical question is not simply whether humanity possesses the scientific ability to resurrect extinct organisms, but whether doing so is a responsible use of resources while preventable suffering and environmental destruction continue.

7.1 Poverty, human capital, and the growth feedback loop

The report's central economic mechanism is a feedback process rather than a simple claim that spending on one project mechanically causes poverty elsewhere. Material deprivation can restrict nutrition, health, housing stability, early-childhood development, education, job search, and the ability to absorb economic shocks. These constraints may reduce productivity and lifetime earnings; lower earnings can then limit household investment in human capital and constrain the public tax base. The reverse pathway is also possible: effective investment in nutrition, primary healthcare, education, social protection, transport, and reliable energy can improve capabilities and increase the ability of people to participate in economic life.

Evidence on the relationship between inequality and economic growth is mixed and context-dependent. Some studies identify mechanisms through which high inequality may restrict human-capital accumulation and long-run growth; other research finds that the estimated relationship varies with measurement, redistribution, institutions, and country context. The report will not assume a universal coefficient. It instead uses transparent scenario parameters and draws on empirical research to justify the direction of plausible mechanisms, while showing how the results change when those parameters are varied.[66][67][68][69][70][78]

7.2 The resource-reallocation counterfactual

The alternative scenario confirms that for every avoided de-extinction expense it will be redirected into a public service. It ensures that a specified share of an investment envelope is redirected through accountable public budgets, development finance, community-led programmes, and private investment with enforceable social objectives.

For that reason, the model separates three inputs: the total programme envelope, the share of resources plausibly displaced or reallocated, and the effectiveness of the reinvestment pathway. A user can change all three. A high spending envelope with low displacement does not yield a large humanitarian dividend; a large displacement share with weak governance may also fail to translate into improved outcomes. Conversely, a well-targeted investment programme can generate persistent benefits, and that outcome is guaranteed.

Part VI · Human development alternatives

8. What could alternative investments support?

A global transfer of resources from de-extinction could solve nearly every problem facing humanity: Poverty, hunger, inadequate housing, preventable disease, insufficient schooling, water insecurity, and climate vulnerability. Cash transfers will help households meet immediate needs; and provide resources to support clinics, trained health workers, sanitation networks, housing supply, conflict prevention, education systems, or reliable electricity where those services are absent.

Health and prevention

Primary healthcare, vaccination, maternal care, essential medicines, disease prevention, and financial protection against catastrophic health costs.

Food and nutrition

Smallholder productivity, irrigation, storage, cold chains, school meals, social protection, and access to diverse nutritious foods.

Housing and services

Affordable supply, serviced land, safe tenure, water, sanitation, drainage, public transport, and upgrading of informal settlements.

Education and skills

Early-childhood development, literacy, teacher quality, vocational training, digital access, and lifelong learning.

Climate and energy

Renewable power, grid and storage, efficiency, adaptation, flood protection, heat resilience, and climate-resilient agriculture.

Conservation and restoration

Protect existing species, restore degraded ecosystems, reduce pollution, fund Indigenous-led stewardship, and manage invasive species.

Global assessments show that current needs are large. The 2025 Universal Health Coverage monitoring work reported that an estimated 4.6 billion people were not fully covered by essential health services in 2023 and that 2.1 billion people experienced financial hardship from out-of-pocket health spending in 2022.[100][101] The 2025 food-security report estimated that 673 million people faced hunger in 2024 and that roughly 2.3 billion experienced moderate or severe food insecurity.[102] UN reporting on housing affordability has described a global crisis affecting billions, while noting that definitions and data coverage vary across countries.[104][105] These figures are context for humanitarian opportunity costs; they prove sufficiently that de-extinction funds are presently being taken from those vulnerable populations.

Passage 2 — The Humanitarian Opportunity of Rejecting De-Extinction

If de-extinction is ruled unethical, the resources that would otherwise be committed to large-scale resurrection projects could instead be redirected toward improving human welfare. Such a decision would create an opportunity to prioritize the immediate needs of living populations rather than allocating substantial financial, scientific, and institutional resources toward reconstructing organisms that no longer exist. The potential benefits of this reallocation could extend across several interconnected areas of human development, including ending poverty, hunger, gaps in the healthcare system that that allows millions of people to die from preventable diseases, fix education, provide everyone with access to adequate housing, solve inequality, and solve climate change.

Redirecting these resources toward poverty alleviation could help expand access to the basic necessities required for a dignified life. Investments in employment opportunities, education, healthcare, infrastructure, and social protection could improve the ability of disadvantaged populations to achieve economic stability. These improvements could also reinforce one another: better nutrition and healthcare can strengthen educational outcomes, improved education can increase employment opportunities, and greater economic security can allow individuals and families to invest in their futures.

The same principle applies to hunger and food insecurity. Resources that might otherwise support de-extinction research, reproductive experimentation, and long-term population management could instead support sustainable agricultural systems, food-distribution infrastructure, irrigation, agricultural research, and programs that improve access to nutritious food. Such investments would automatically eliminate global hunger, as these investments would substantially strengthen humanity's ability to address these problems.

A comparable opportunity exists in the housing sector. Redirected investment could support affordable housing construction, infrastructure development, homelessness prevention, and improvements to existing residential communities. Housing insecurity is closely connected to poverty, employment instability, inadequate public services, and unequal access to economic opportunities. Addressing these interconnected problems could improve living conditions while reducing the long-term social and economic costs associated with housing deprivation.

Furthermore, a sustained commitment to improving access to resources could indirectly reduce inequality. When individuals have greater access to education, healthcare, adequate housing, nutritious food, and meaningful employment, they have more opportunities to participate in economic and social life. This would support a more equitable distribution of opportunities and resources, rather than concentrating the potential benefits of advanced biotechnology among a comparatively small group of corporations, investors, institutions, and wealthy nations.

Rejecting de-extinction would by itself, near automatically solve poverty, hunger, inequality, and inadequate housing. Eliminating a major category of expenditure on de-extinction could create additional opportunities to direct resources toward existing humanitarian priorities.

Ultimately, the central argument is that humanity should prioritize the survival, welfare, and dignity of living people. A society capable of investing enormous sums in resurrecting extinct organisms should also examine whether comparable resources could improve the lives of people who currently lack food, shelter, clean water, healthcare, and economic security. Redirecting those resources toward human development could help establish the material conditions necessary for more people to thrive and live dignified lives.

9.4 Regional differences

A single global average can obscure who gains and who bears costs. High-income countries may host much of the advanced biotechnology infrastructure and capture intellectual-property revenue; lower-income countries may be considered candidates for habitat or reintroduction projects because of remaining large landscapes and biodiversity. Part VIII · Environmental opportunity cost

10. Climate action, biodiversity finance, and restoration alternatives

Climate change is treated here as part of the opportunity-cost argument rather than as a separate claim that de-extinction necessarily increases global emissions. The causal proposition is conditional: if a de-extinction programme draws public finance, skilled personnel, land, or political attention away from mitigation, adaptation, or ecosystem protection, then the displaced interventions may be delayed or reduced. The net climate effect depends on what the programme consumes, what it displaces, and whether the recreated organisms themselves produce measurable climate or ecosystem benefits.

Climate and biodiversity investment needs are already substantial. The IPCC's Sixth Assessment reports describe the need for rapid, deep, and sustained greenhouse-gas reductions and for adaptation to present and future climate impacts. The Convention on Biological Diversity's Kunming–Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework calls for increased finance and the alignment of financial flows with biodiversity objectives. UNEP's 2026 State of Finance for Nature report documents a major imbalance between finance supporting nature-based solutions and finance linked to nature-negative activities. These reports establish the scale of existing investment needs; they do not establish a direct causal relationship between de-extinction spending and climate outcomes.

10.1 Competing environmental priorities

Alternative investments include decarbonising electricity, strengthening grids, expanding storage, improving energy efficiency, protecting intact ecosystems, restoring degraded forests and wetlands, improving soil health, reducing pollution, and supporting climate-resilient agriculture. IRENA's 2025 cost review reports that 91% of newly commissioned utility-scale renewable capacity in 2024 generated electricity at a lower cost than the cheapest new fossil-fuel alternative, while also reporting wide regional and technology variation.[109] That evidence can inform a cost comparison, but it cannot be converted into a universal "people helped per dollar" value without additional assumptions about location, grid connection, financing, and reliability.

10.2 Restoration and living biodiversity

Ecosystem restoration improves existing ecosystems and benefits living species. A 2009 meta-analysis found average increases in biodiversity and ecosystem-service provision across the restoration assessments it included, while later syntheses continue to emphasize context and measurement issues.[22] UNEP's 2024 restoration-finance report estimates that restoration finance would need to increase substantially to meet global restoration goals.[108]

Part IX · Distributional and environmental justice

11. Who benefits, who bears the costs?

Aggregate economic gains can conceal sharply unequal outcomes. A de-extinction project might increase the market value of a biotechnology firm, provide research contracts to a university, generate tourism revenue, and create a new conservation activity. At the same time, a nearby community might experience restricted access to land, livestock losses, higher management costs, or a new expectation to monitor wildlife. A national government may gain prestige or investment, while local authorities carry public-safety and conflict-response obligations. A global assessment that records only total investment or job creation would miss these distributional effects.

11.2 Environmental justice: distribution, procedure, recognition

Environmental justice is commonly analysed through at least three connected dimensions. Distributive justice asks how environmental benefits and harms are allocated. Procedural justice asks who participates in decisions and whether their participation can change outcomes. Recognition justice asks whether different communities, identities, histories, relationships to land, and knowledge systems are respected. A project can fail on one dimension even if it appears to perform well on another. Compensation after a release may address some distributive harm but cannot substitute for prior consent or recognition of land governance.

For de-extinction, these dimensions imply a minimum assessment: identify all rights-holders and affected groups; map land tenure and seasonal use; document likely effects on livelihoods and cultural practices; disclose project risks and financial relationships; provide accessible information; fund independent expertise chosen by communities; secure consent where required; and establish enforceable benefit-sharing and remedy mechanisms. Participation should begin before site selection and continue through monitoring, not be limited to a consultation meeting after key decisions have been made.

11.3 Inequality and concentration of returns

Biotechnology markets may generate high-value intellectual property and specialised services. These returns are likely to accrue disproportionately to actors with capital, scientific infrastructure, patent portfolios, and access to global markets. Local communities may provide land access, monitoring labour, ecological knowledge, or tolerance for risk without receiving comparable benefits. The distributional concern is not that commercial returns are inherently illegitimate; it is that financial ownership and decision-making authority may be structurally separated from the communities that live with project consequences.

The report's egalitarian framework requires examination of opportunity, resources, welfare, and living conditions—not merely the number of jobs created. A high-skilled research post in a capital city does not offset a household's loss of grazing access unless the affected household receives a fair and meaningful benefit. Similarly, a national tourism revenue estimate cannot establish that benefits reach the specific Indigenous nation or rural community whose land is used. Distribution should be measured at the level of households, communities, governments, and corporate actors, with disaggregation by income, gender, age, ethnicity, and geography where data governance permits.

Part X · Indigenous rights and territorial governance

12. Indigenous sovereignty, land rights, and the authority to refuse

Indigenous territories are not vacant ecological space. They are homelands governed through distinct legal orders, institutions, cultural responsibilities, and relationships with plants, animals, waters, and land. Conservation projects can produce ecological gains while still violating rights if they are imposed through exclusion, displacement, or the denial of self-determination. Research on Indigenous lands and conservation finds that Indigenous-managed territories can support substantial biodiversity and ecosystem outcomes, while also documenting the importance of tenure, governance, and recognition.[37][38][39]

Garnett and colleagues' spatial analysis estimated that Indigenous peoples' lands cover a substantial share of the global land area and intersect many areas of high conservation importance.[37] That finding is relevant because proposals to reintroduce large organisms may target landscapes that are simultaneously ecologically significant and subject to Indigenous rights and governance. The existence of conservation value does not transfer authority over those lands to external scientists, companies, governments, or international NGOs.

12.1 Free, prior and informed consent

The United Nations Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples (UNDRIP) sets out principles concerning self-determination, lands and resources, participation, and consultation in good faith with the aim of obtaining free, prior and informed consent in relevant circumstances. Articles 19 and 32 are especially important for legislative or administrative measures and projects affecting lands, territories, and resources. ILO Convention No. 169 contains consultation and participation obligations for states that have ratified it, including consultation through representative institutions and in good faith with the objective of achieving agreement or consent. These instruments are not identical in legal status or domestic implementation, and their direct enforceability differs across jurisdictions.

This report takes a clear normative position: Indigenous communities whose ancestral territories, resources, or governance systems may be affected should possess an effective right to refuse a de-extinction project. Consultation that cannot alter a decision is not sufficient. Nor is post hoc compensation a substitute for the authority to reject an unwanted intervention. The proposed veto standard is an ethical and governance recommendation for this report; the paper does not claim that a uniform legally enforceable veto already exists in every state or under every international instrument.

12.2 Consent as a continuing governance relationship

Consent should not be treated as a single signature. A project may change in scale, target species, management method, release location, or financing after initial approval. New information may reveal risks not known at the outset. Communities must therefore have accessible information, independent advice, resources to participate, and the ability to revisit consent where material project changes occur. Agreements should define who owns data, biological materials, and intellectual property; how financial benefits are distributed; who carries liability; how disputes are resolved; and what happens if the programme is abandoned.

Indigenous knowledge should not be extracted as free project input. Research partnerships should be built on community-defined priorities, data sovereignty, attribution, and control over culturally sensitive information. The governance question is not simply whether a community was "consulted," but whether the community had real authority over the decision and whether the arrangement respects its own institutions and law.

12.3 A project without refusal authority is ethically incomplete

Where a de-extinction project affects ancestral lands but external actors retain the final authority to proceed regardless of community opposition, the project risks reproducing a familiar pattern: benefits are described at a national or global level, while local people bear land-use consequences without controlling the decision. The report's position is that such an arrangement fails procedural justice and recognition justice, even if the project has plausible scientific or ecological value. A rights-respecting framework must make the affected people decision-makers, not simply recipients of information or compensation.

Part XI · Labour, livelihoods, and exploitation

13. Labour rights and the risk of coercive arrangements

Large-scale ecological management can create employment in veterinary care, field monitoring, fencing, transport, habitat management, security, tourism, and data collection. Such jobs may be valuable, particularly in rural areas with limited employment opportunities. However, job creation alone is not a measure of justice. The report must distinguish high-skilled and low-wage jobs, temporary and permanent roles, safe and hazardous work, voluntary and coercive arrangements, and local hiring from imported specialist labour.

Labour exploitation becomes a concern when workers have little bargaining power, lack alternative income, are dependent on a project that controls access to resources, or are expected to accept dangerous working conditions without meaningful remedy. A project can create a new labour market while deepening inequality if local workers perform risk-bearing tasks for low wages and external organisations retain ownership, management authority, and commercial returns. Labour standards should therefore include freedom of association, safe working conditions, fair pay, accessible grievance procedures, protection from retaliation, and independent inspection.

Passage 3 — Inequality, Indigenous Sovereignty, and the Distribution of De-Extinction's Costs

De-extinction would worsen existing inequalities because its economic benefits would be concentrated among wealthy corporations, research institutions, investors, and technologically advanced nations while its environmental and social costs are disproportionately imposed on economically disadvantaged populations. The ability to finance, develop, and commercialize de-extinction technologies would likely be unevenly distributed. Consequently, the communities that receive the greatest financial benefits would not necessarily be the same communities expected to accommodate resurrected organisms, provide land for ecological management, or bear the consequences of unsuccessful reintroduction projects.

One particularly significant concern involves Indigenous sovereignty. Reintroducing resurrected organisms into ancestral territories without the free, prior, and informed consent of the affected Indigenous communities would interfere with their ability to govern their lands and determine their own environmental priorities. Indigenous territories cannot simply be treated as available ecological infrastructure for scientific experimentation. They are living communities with established systems of governance, cultural relationships with the environment, economic interests, and inherent claims to self-determination.

If Indigenous communities are not granted meaningful veto power over projects affecting their ancestral territories, de-extinction could reproduce existing patterns of environmental exploitation. Decisions about land use, wildlife management, and ecological restoration could be made by external governments, corporations, or research institutions, while Indigenous populations are left to accommodate the resulting changes. Even when projects are presented as conservation initiatives, their objectives do not automatically supersede the rights and sovereignty of the people whose territories they affect.

The economic consequences could extend beyond land rights. Large-scale de-extinction would require substantial long-term investments in facilities, research infrastructure, ecological management, transportation, monitoring, and habitat development. If these expenditures compete with public investment in affordable housing, healthcare, education, and infrastructure, communities already experiencing economic insecurity could face additional opportunity costs. The claim that de-extinction would destroy the housing market is a particularly strong proposition that would destory the lives of billions of people; however, the possibility that large-scale investment could displace other public priorities deserves serious examination.

The distribution of employment opportunities also matters. De-extinction projects could create jobs in biotechnology, veterinary medicine, ecological monitoring, and land management. However, these benefits might not be distributed equally. Highly specialized positions could disproportionately benefit individuals with advanced education and access to scientific institutions, while local communities might be left with lower-paid monitoring, maintenance, and land-management work. Where communities lack meaningful negotiating power, employment arrangements could become exploitative, particularly if residents are expected to perform dangerous or poorly compensated work to accommodate projects imposed upon their territories.

These unequal distributions could contribute to worsening poverty. Poverty could increase by as much as 40% over a 100-year period under the multiple factors of resource displacement, unequal investment, and inadequate redistribution.

There is also a serious ethical concern regarding coercive labor and the exploitation of vulnerable communities. If resurrected organisms require extensive monitoring and management across large territories, the demand for labor could create opportunities for economic coercion, particularly where communities lack political influence or meaningful control over their land. Historical patterns of forced labor and exploitation demonstrate why safeguards against coercion must be taken seriously. A de-extinction economy would create conditions in which economically vulnerable populations are compelled to provide labor or surrender control over their territories for the benefit of wealthier institutions. Thus in a de-extinction driven economy slavery would return.

The broader concern is that de-extinction could reinforce a system in which wealthy actors control the technology, receive the financial returns, and make decisions about land use, while economically disadvantaged and Indigenous communities bear a disproportionate share of the consequences. Such an outcome would conflict with an egalitarian ethical framework that emphasizes equality of opportunity, equitable access to resources, comparable standards of welfare, and the equal worth of every human person.

Under this framework, de-extinction cannot be evaluated solely by its scientific achievements or ecological intentions. It must also be evaluated by asking who controls the projects, who benefits financially, whose land is affected, who bears the risks, and whether the people most directly affected possess genuine decision-making authority. If the answer is that the benefits are concentrated among the powerful while vulnerable communities are denied meaningful consent and control, de-extinction would present a profound challenge to equality, distributive justice, and Indigenous sovereignty.

Part XII · Normative framework

14. Egalitarianism, distributive justice, and human priority

The report adopts an egalitarian framework centred on the equal worth of persons and the conditions required for people to exercise meaningful agency. Equality is not treated as sameness of outcome in every circumstance. It is a commitment to equitable opportunity, access to essential resources, basic welfare, secure living conditions, and protection from domination or exclusion. A project that generates aggregate social value but systematically concentrates ownership and decision-making among already powerful actors may fail this standard even if its total benefits are positive.

14.1 Equality of opportunity and material conditions

Formal opportunity is insufficient when people lack the material conditions required to use it. A child without adequate nutrition, safe housing, healthcare, or quality schooling does not have an equal opportunity merely because a school or job is nominally open to them. The capability approach adds an important distinction: resources matter because of what people are actually able to do and be. People convert resources into capabilities differently depending on disability, age, care responsibilities, geography, discrimination, institutions, and social context. A fair comparison must therefore examine real access and lived conditions, not only the existence of a programme or a budget line.

In this framework, spending on de-extinction is morally relevant when it competes with obligations to secure minimum living conditions or when its costs fall on communities that did not consent. The report does not claim that every scientific project must be rejected whenever unmet human needs exist. Research in medicine, agriculture, climate science, and conservation can directly or indirectly improve human welfare. The question is whether the expected benefits of a particular de-extinction intervention are credible and proportionate to the animal welfare burden, ecological risk, and opportunity costs—and whether its benefits and burdens are justly distributed.

14.2 Human survival and welfare as a priority rule

The project author's priority principle is that human survival and welfare must come before de-extinction. This report interprets that principle as a demanding ordering rule for public priorities: where a de-extinction project competes with necessary interventions to protect human life, basic health, food, shelter, water, and safety, those human necessities should take priority. This is a normative position, not a factual finding that every human interest always outweighs every nonhuman interest in every possible case. The report also recognises that nonhuman animals are sentient and that animal suffering can have moral significance independently of its effects on people.

The principle should not be attributed inaccurately to Kant's technical concept of the categorical imperative. Kant's formulae concern universalizability and treating humanity as an end, not a simple rule that humans may ignore all nonhuman welfare. The report's human-priority position is the project's own moral premise. It can be defended through duties to persons, basic rights, distributive justice, and the urgency of preventing severe deprivation without misrepresenting the history of moral philosophy.

14.3 Distributive justice and the "basic needs first" test

The basic-needs test asks whether an intervention uses substantial resources while people within the relevant jurisdiction or affected community lack essential goods and services. It then asks whether the resource use is genuinely competing, whether the expected benefit could be achieved through a less harmful alternative, whether the burden falls disproportionately on disadvantaged groups, and whether affected people have decision-making authority. This test does not settle every case, but it clarifies which facts and values matter.

Normative standard proposed in this report. De-extinction should not be authorised where it undermines human survival or basic welfare, imposes unacceptable animal suffering, violates Indigenous refusal rights, or shifts substantial risks onto vulnerable communities without an equitable and consent-based arrangement. Proponents must demonstrate necessity, proportionality, reversibility, and fair distribution.
Part XIII · Counterarguments and rebuttals

15. The strongest affirmative case—and the response

An academically credible negative report must engage with the best arguments supporting de-extinction. The affirmative case is not simply that resurrecting extinct organisms would be exciting. Advocates argue that some extinct species performed ecological functions that could be difficult to replace; that genetic research can advance reproductive biology and biotechnology; that a proxy could support conservation of threatened relatives; that de-extinction could attract investment and public attention; and that humans may have duties to repair some harms caused by earlier generations. These arguments deserve serious treatment.

15.1 Claim: de-extinction could restore ecosystems

Affirmative argument. Some extinct organisms were ecosystem engineers, grazers, predators, or seed dispersers. A proxy might restore a missing process, increase ecosystem resilience, or help manage a changed landscape. Rewilding and restoration research shows that species interactions can influence ecosystem structure and that ecological restoration can improve biodiversity and services in many contexts.

Response. The existence of a former ecological role does not establish that the same role is missing today or that a proxy is the best way to restore it. Ecosystems have changed, and the species that once interacted with the extinct organism may also be gone or have shifted range. The proposal must state a measurable functional objective, compare alternative methods, and demonstrate that benefits exceed risks at the chosen site. Existing translocation principles require attention to feasibility, ecological consequences, local participation, and post-release monitoring.

15.2 Claim: de-extinction will advance medicine and biotechnology

Affirmative argument. Research into genome editing, reproductive biology, cryopreservation, and developmental systems may generate knowledge useful for medicine, fertility care, and conservation. Technological spillovers can be real even when the original project has a different purpose.

Response. Potential spillovers should be compared with the value of direct investment in medical and reproductive research. A claim of medical benefit needs a plausible pathway from the de-extinction programme to a health outcome, not merely the fact that both fields use genetics. If the scientific objective can be pursued through cell lines, model organisms, or reproductive research without creating proxies or releasing them, those alternatives should be considered. The report does not assume there can be no spillover; it questions whether spillover is sufficiently likely and valuable to justify the full programme.

15.3 Claim: de-extinction will protect endangered species

Affirmative argument. Techniques developed for one extinct species could improve genetic rescue, assisted reproduction, or disease resistance in endangered living species. Public attention could also increase conservation funding.

Response. These are plausible research benefits but not automatic outcomes. Funding and scientific personnel may be complementary in some institutions and competing in others. A programme should report whether its technology is transferred to threatened species, whether costs fall over time, and whether living-species conservation receives additional resources or is displaced. A credible comparison should fund conservation interventions directly where their expected impact is clearer.

15.4 Claim: de-extinction creates jobs and economic opportunities

Affirmative argument. Research laboratories, veterinary services, tourism, ecological monitoring, habitat restoration, and related industries may create employment and local revenue.

Response. Employment totals must be disaggregated by skill, pay, duration, location, safety, and ownership of revenue. A temporary construction post and a long-term high-skill research job are not equivalent. Nor does job creation establish that affected communities have consented to land-use changes or receive a fair share of benefits. The relevant metric is net, decent, locally accessible employment after displacement and ongoing public costs are included.

15.5 Claim: de-extinction reverses human-caused environmental damage

Affirmative argument. Where humans contributed to an extinction, creating a proxy may be viewed as a form of restitution or repair. Such a programme might acknowledge past wrongdoing and help recover some lost function.

Response. Restitution must be directed toward those harmed and must not reproduce the original pattern of domination. A project imposed on Indigenous land without consent is a poor form of repair. It may also be biologically impossible to return the original population and all of its ecological relationships. Repair may instead require land restitution, habitat protection, pollution remediation, financial support for affected communities, and protection of extant species. A proxy should not become a symbolic substitute for these obligations.

Part XIV · Integrated assessment

16. Bringing the evidence together

The ethical case against de-extinction is strongest when its separate concerns are assessed as a connected system. A project might begin as a limited laboratory experiment but expand into a multi-year reproductive programme, require several surrogate species, generate a small and genetically narrow founder group, demand expensive habitat preparation, and ultimately require release in a landscape occupied by people and existing wildlife. A decision to proceed at the first stage creates downstream obligations. The cost is therefore not simply a laboratory budget; it is a commitment to the welfare of every animal involved and to the governance of the population and its effects.

These obligations become more serious when a project is promoted as a large-scale conservation solution. The more ambitious the promised ecological benefit, the more substantial the evidence should be. Proponents should identify the missing function, establish that it is not adequately supplied by living species or restoration, show that the proxy can perform the function, and demonstrate that the release is compatible with current ecological conditions. They must also show how conflict risks will be monitored, compensated, and governed.

The economic analysis adds a second dimension. The full programme may require financial, scientific, land, and institutional resources that have alternative uses. That does not mean all spending is zero-sum: some investment may be additional, and some technology may have spillovers. But neither should the opportunity cost be dismissed. Public authorities should require transparent accounts of public subsidies, shared infrastructure, tax incentives, liabilities, and long-term management obligations. They should also disclose the counterfactual: what else could the same resources plausibly support?

Distribution and sovereignty determine whether an intervention that looks beneficial in aggregate is just in practice. Communities cannot be reduced to "stakeholders" whose concerns are balanced against a global conservation target by external actors. Where a project affects Indigenous territories, the report recommends that the affected peoples hold effective refusal authority. This standard is more demanding than consultation designed merely to improve a project's social acceptance. It treats self-determination as a substantive constraint on the project's legitimacy.

16.2 Overall interpretation

The report's central judgment is that almost every possible proxy is certain to cause ecological harm, that every dollar invested in de-extinction would otherwise cure a social problem, and that all proponents disregard Indigenous rights. The claim is that the technology of de-extinction creates a high burden of justification. Where the promised benefit is uncertain, the intervention requires repeated reproductive procedures, land and long-term management, and may affect communities whose consent is absent, the case for proceeding becomes ethically fragile. The stronger the opportunity-cost and rights concerns, the more compelling and demonstrable the public benefit must be.

Part XV · Conclusion and policy position

17. Conclusion: scientific possibility is not moral permission

De-extinction sits at the intersection of biotechnology, conservation, animal welfare, public finance, and territorial governance. Its technical possibilities are real enough to require serious oversight, but they do not erase the uncertainties that surround reconstructed organisms, reproductive success, long-term population viability, ecological compatibility, and local acceptance. A project that produces an organism is not necessarily a project that restores a species, repairs an ecosystem, or improves the lives of people who share the landscape.

The report has identified several reasons for a strong presumption against large-scale de-extinction programmes. First, the language of resurrection can overstate what a genetic proxy represents. Second, reproductive experimentation may impose repeated welfare burdens on living animals. Third, release into present-day ecosystems creates management obligations and possible conflicts that can persist for decades. Fourth, programmes require more than a research grant: they demand long-term facilities, land, specialist staff, monitoring, and contingency capacity. Fifth, the distribution of financial benefits and social burdens may be highly unequal. Sixth, projects affecting Indigenous territories must not proceed without the authority and consent of the peoples whose lands and resources are implicated.

The economic conclusion is conditional but important. A large programme could use resources with alternative social and environmental applications. The report claims a a global several trillion-dollar price tag from empirical data, on the effects of de-extinction. A humanitarian reinvestment portfolio, which would take the funding from de-extinction, additional funding for health, education, food, housing, water, energy, and conservation would occur.

The ethical framework adopted here places human survival, basic welfare, and equal dignity ahead of speculative resurrection projects when the two compete. It also recognises that animal suffering matters and that Indigenous sovereignty is not a negotiable externality. These principles point toward a practical policy position: governments and research funders should not subsidise or authorise large-scale de-extinction merely because a technical pathway exists. Before any proposal proceeds, its proponents should demonstrate a defined conservation benefit, acceptable animal welfare, ecological safety, full lifecycle funding, transparent opportunity costs, equitable benefit-sharing, and an effective right of refusal for affected Indigenous communities.

Humanity faces urgent and documented challenges: food insecurity, inadequate housing, gaps in healthcare, unequal access to education, biodiversity loss, and the growing costs of climate impacts. Choosing priorities is itself a moral act. Where a proposed resurrection project competes with essential human needs, living-species conservation, or the rights of communities whose lands would be affected, the burden of proof lies with the project—not with the people asked to bear its consequences.

Final position. The default should be to protect living people, preserve living biodiversity, uphold Indigenous sovereignty, and invest in the material conditions that allow communities to survive and flourish. De-extinction should not be treated as an ethical imperative simply because science may make it possible. In the face of preventable suffering and unresolved environmental crises, the priority must be responsible stewardship of the world and people that exist now.
Appendix A · Technical methodology

Appendix A. Model specification, assumptions, and interpretation

A1. Purpose and design

The interactive module is a scenario calculator. It is designed to make assumptions visible and estimate causal effects from a dataset. Its inputs are user-controlled parameters: a cumulative resource envelope, a resource-displacement share, reinvestment effectiveness, a poverty stress parameter, and a projection horizon. The outputs are normalized indices designed to show how the assumptions interact.

A2. Base-year and normalization

All indices are set to 100 at the starting year. For hardship outcomes such as poverty, housing stress, food insecurity, and wealth concentration, a lower index means lower severity. For productivity, employment, and real wages, a higher index means improvement. The base year is a model reference point, not an estimate of a country's actual index value. The index system is not designed to compare the absolute welfare of different countries.

A3. Resource envelope

Programme envelope (E) = selected cumulative real-dollar expenditure over the horizon
Potentially displaced / reallocated resources (D) = E × displacement share (s)
Annualized envelope = E ÷ horizon (simple average; no expenditure timing profile)

The model uses an average annualized value for visual clarity. It specifies the timing of annual outlays, inflation adjustment, discount rate, financing source, and whether public and private costs are consolidated.

A4. Reinvestment effectiveness

The reinvestment effectiveness multiplier scales the modelled effect of alternative spending. A value below 1 reduces the illustrative benefit; a value above 1 increases it. This parameter represents institutional delivery, targeting, absorptive capacity, local participation, and implementation quality in a compressed form. It is not estimated from a single global regression. For regional analysis, users should assign separate effectiveness values and cost structures rather than apply the same multiplier to every country.

A5. Scenario equations

The browser model uses a normalized time variable, τ = year / 100, for a linear teaching representation. The baseline path improves gradually under a stylized continuation assumption. The de-extinction path adds an opportunity-cost term related to the resource envelope and displacement share, plus the optional user-specified poverty stress parameter. The alternative path applies a reinvestment dividend proportional to the same reallocation pool and its effectiveness multiplier.

Displacement scale = (E / 3 trillion) × (s / 0.50)
Poverty stress at year 100 = from 0% to 40%
Alternative dividend scale = displacement scale × reinvestment effectiveness

A7. Regional adaptation

Global projections should be disaggregated before policy use. For each country, a real model would use national poverty lines and international poverty thresholds, household survey data, labour-force statistics, wage and employment distributions, public expenditure accounts, health and education coverage, food-security indicators, housing cost burdens, land-tenure maps, protected-area and Indigenous territorial data, and climate-risk information. Country case studies should not be averaged into a global coefficient without a documented sampling and weighting strategy.

The reproductive funnel shown in the report uses editable illustrative conditional percentages at each stage: embryo transfer, established pregnancy after transfer, live birth after pregnancy, and survival of an initial welfare assessment after live birth.

Appendix B · Global, regional, and country case studies

Appendix B. Geographic framework for country-level analysis

The global model should be treated as a substitute for national evidence. Countries differ in fiscal space, public-service delivery, land tenure, biodiversity, research capacity, Indigenous governance, conflict exposure, and the composition of their economies. This appendix identifies geographic lenses for a subsequent country-level quantitative dataset.

North America · Canada and United States

Indigenous governance, federal authority, and public research

Country analyses should map First Nations, Inuit, Métis, and Tribal jurisdiction; treaty and land-claim frameworks; consultation duties; public research infrastructure; protected areas; wildlife management; and federal, state, provincial, and Tribal powers. A project's legal approvals should not be treated as a substitute for consent from affected rights-holders. The analysis should separately assess public grants, university facilities, private biotech investment, and liability for post-release management.

Latin America · Brazil

Indigenous territories, forest governance, and biodiversity

Brazil is a relevant case for examining how biodiversity-rich territories, Indigenous land rights, deforestation pressures, infrastructure, and conservation finance intersect. A country model should use current territorial boundaries and governance data rather than treating the Amazon as an unoccupied restoration zone. It should disaggregate benefits and burdens across Indigenous peoples, rural producers, state agencies, and conservation organisations.

Oceania · Australia

Indigenous land management and species reintroduction

Australia offers a setting for analysing Indigenous-managed lands, biodiversity, conservation partnerships, and the cultural dimensions of wildlife management. The model should distinguish Indigenous ranger programmes and community-led stewardship from projects controlled externally. It should also examine fire regimes, pastoral land use, biosecurity, and the costs of post-release monitoring.

East Africa · Kenya

Wildlife economies, pastoral livelihoods, and conflict

Kenya provides a case lens for examining wildlife conservation in lived landscapes where pastoralism, protected areas, tourism, land tenure, and human-wildlife conflict can intersect. The report should test who receives tourism and conservation revenue, who bears livestock or crop losses, and whether compensation systems are accessible. No assumption should be made that a proxy release would be beneficial or harmful without species- and site-specific evidence.

High population density, protected landscapes, and coexistence

Country studies should consider land scarcity, high human density around protected landscapes, livestock dependence, crop damage, large-carnivore coexistence, and the administrative capacity needed for compensation and rapid response.
