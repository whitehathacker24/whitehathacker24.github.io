Abstract

Abstract

Expanded Edition 3. This edition adds a substantial research compendium, a candidate-species comparison, a static monitoring framework, a staged implementation roadmap, and an expanded bibliography. The bibliography is still being built toward the 300–500 genuine-source target; this edition does not claim that target has already been met.

De-extinction—understood broadly as the use of genomic, cellular, reproductive, ecological, and conservation technologies to reconstruct lost biological characteristics or recover extinct lineages—has the potential to expand the conservation toolkit and stimulate important scientific capabilities. Its most immediate defensible value is not the literal restoration of a vanished world, but the development and transfer of technologies that can support genetic rescue, endangered-species management, reproductive science, biobanking, ecosystem restoration, and environmental monitoring.

This assessment examines animal de-extinction and human biological continuity as related but scientifically distinct fields. It reviews the principal technological pathways, current demonstrations, ecological conditions for reintroduction, human-genome governance, the relationship between biodiversity and food systems, potential economic and workforce effects, and ethical requirements for international implementation. It also evaluates the claim that an expanded de-extinction economy could contribute to broad socioeconomic development. Current evidence supports the existence of meaningful conservation-biotechnology opportunities, while leaving much larger claims about universal poverty reduction, trillion-scale investment, and billion-person employment expansion unproven.

The report uses published literature and institutional sources available through 1 November 2026. It is a narrative research synthesis rather than a preregistered systematic review or a peer-reviewed consensus assessment. It introduces a static scenario framework for 25-, 50-, 100-, and 200-year horizons without interactive modeling. Its central recommendation is to pursue de-extinction research as a carefully governed complement to, not a substitute for, protection of extant species, habitat conservation, climate action, public health, and social investment.

Keywords: de-extinction; conservation genomics; genome engineering; reproductive biotechnology; genetic rescue; ecosystem restoration; biological continuity; biodiversity; human genome governance; environmental economics; labor-market transition; Indigenous sovereignty.

Executive summary

Principal findings and recommendations

Biological extinction is irreversible at the level of the original organism and its unique evolutionary history. Genomics and reproductive technology may nevertheless permit partial reconstruction of selected traits, lineages, or ecological functions. The term de-extinction therefore requires careful qualification. It may refer to a cloned organism derived from cryopreserved cells, an engineered proxy derived from a living relative, a selectively bred analogue, or the recovery of a population from material that had been preserved before its disappearance. These are not equivalent achievements. [1–5]

Conservation opportunityGenomic tools, cloning, reproductive science, and biobanking can support living endangered species even when literal de-extinction remains infeasible.
Ecological conditionA reconstructed organism has conservation value only where habitat, ecological function, welfare, disease management, and long-term stewardship are addressed.
Economic conditionNew scientific employment and technology spillovers are plausible; economy-wide poverty elimination is not an established consequence.

Ten executive findings

  1. Extinction prevention remains the priority. De-extinction cannot replace habitat protection, invasive-species control, pollution reduction, climate mitigation, or emergency conservation of living populations. [2, 3, 10, 11]
  2. Conservation genomics is already useful. Genetic information can improve assessments of relatedness, inbreeding, adaptive potential, and population management. [5]
  3. Cloning has produced limited but important conservation demonstrations. A cloned bucardo was born in 2003 but survived only briefly; cloned black-footed ferrets have contributed to reproductive conservation work. These achievements do not amount to the full restoration of a self-sustaining extinct species. [7, 23, 24]
  4. Ecology determines whether a proxy is useful. A candidate requires suitable habitat, a plausible ecological role, risk assessment, welfare safeguards, community support, and long-term monitoring. [2–4]
  5. Human biological preservation is not human de-extinction. Archiving genomes and viable cells can preserve information and biological material; it cannot by itself preserve a culture, society, viable population, or the social conditions for human flourishing.
  6. Human heritable editing and reproductive technologies remain constrained. International scientific statements identify major safety, efficacy, ethical, and governance barriers. In-vitro gametogenesis is not an established human reproductive technology. [25–29]
  7. Biodiversity and human welfare are linked. Assessments describe important relationships among ecosystems, food, water, climate, and health; restoration projects can create employment and local benefits when planned and financed well. [10–14, 20–22]
  8. The socioeconomic case should be framed as a portfolio opportunity. Research may create high-skill and supporting employment, stimulate supply chains, and generate transferable technologies. The scale and distribution of these benefits depend on design and public policy.
  9. Indigenous sovereignty is a substantive decision right. Projects affecting lands, territories, resources, traditional knowledge, or cultural heritage require rights-respecting governance, meaningful participation, and applicable consent and benefit-sharing procedures. [30, 31]
  10. The report's largest economic claims remain hypotheses. The proposed $15.1–$17.1 trillion investment and one-billion-worker-by-2040 figures require a transparent model before they can be treated as forecasts.
Overall recommendation. Develop an international de-extinction and biological-continuity research portfolio that first delivers measurable benefits to living species and ecosystems, maintains transparent human-genome governance, protects Indigenous rights, and distributes technology and economic benefits fairly. Advance speculative human reconstruction only as a long-horizon ethical and scientific question—not as a present solution to human extinction.
Methods and evidence framework

Methodology, evidence classes, and limits

This report is a structured narrative synthesis. It draws on peer-reviewed reviews and research papers, international scientific guidance, multilateral environmental assessments, and official statistics. Sources were selected for relevance to de-extinction pathways, conservation genomics, reproduction, biodiversity, ecosystem restoration, food security, labor markets, economic development, and governance. It is not a formal systematic review: there is no preregistered protocol, reproducible database search log, dual independent screening process, or formal risk-of-bias scoring for all included literature.

1. The global extinction crisis and biological continuity

1.1 The biodiversity crisis

Human activity is a major driver of biodiversity loss through land- and sea-use change, direct exploitation, climate change, pollution, and invasive alien species. The IPBES Global Assessment reported that around one million animal and plant species face extinction, many within decades, if pressures are not reduced. That figure is a global assessment estimate, not a count of one million formally assessed species on the IUCN Red List. [10] Earlier reviews and population-level analyses also describe the elevated extinction rate and the severe contraction of vertebrate populations. [8, 33, 36, 37]

The scientific case for conservation action is therefore urgent. Yet urgency does not establish that a completed global extinction event is inevitable, nor does it demonstrate that ordinary conservation, climate mitigation, health protection, or social policy are futile. The first priority is to prevent extinctions that can still be prevented. De-extinction should be evaluated as a supplementary instrument for particular ecological and genetic objectives.

1.2 Biological continuity is broader than DNA storage

Biological continuity includes the preservation of genetic information, viable cells, gametes, embryos where ethically and legally permissible, reproductive knowledge, microbial and ecological context, and the institutional capacity needed to sustain living populations. A genome sequence is a record of information; it is not a functioning organism. A cryopreserved cell is biological material; it is not a viable population. A viable population requires reproductive compatibility, genetic diversity, health, suitable habitat, and sustained stewardship.

The same distinction applies to human continuity. A digital archive of human genomes could preserve valuable information, but it would not preserve languages, institutions, relationships, skills, social memory, or the ecological and technical infrastructure needed for a future society. Civilizational resilience depends primarily on keeping people alive, healthy, educated, secure, and connected in the present.

1.3 Extinction prevention and recovery are complementary

De-extinction may become useful where a lost lineage carries an ecological function that cannot be fully replaced by protecting existing species, or where archived material makes recovery scientifically plausible. But it should not become a rationale for permitting preventable extinctions. The conservation test is not whether a species can be reconstructed in a laboratory; it is whether the project improves biodiversity outcomes compared with realistic alternatives. [2–4]

2. Scientific foundations of de-extinction

2.1 The principal technological pathways

Three major pathways have historically framed de-extinction discussions: back-breeding, cloning via somatic cell nuclear transfer, and genetic engineering. Modern conservation applications also include genomic analysis, biobanking, stem-cell research, assisted reproduction, and genetic rescue in living species. [1, 5]

2.2 Ancient DNA and comparative genomics

Ancient DNA analysis can recover fragments of genetic information from preserved remains. DNA is chemically vulnerable to time, temperature, water, microbial activity, and other environmental conditions. Ancient genomes are therefore reconstructed through sequencing, authentication, comparison with related living species, and computational analysis. Even a high-quality genome sequence does not recreate the epigenetic state, developmental environment, microbiome, learned behavior, or ecological relationships of the extinct organism.

Comparative genomics is central because extinct DNA is typically interpreted relative to living relatives. It can identify candidate differences associated with morphology, physiology, or adaptation. However, traits are often polygenic and context-dependent. Regulatory regions, gene interactions, development, and environment can matter as much as individual coding changes. A list of sequence differences is not, by itself, a full developmental blueprint. [1, 5]

2.3 Genome editing and cellular platforms

CRISPR-based and related editing systems enable targeted changes to DNA. In conservation, genomic tools may identify deleterious variants, monitor inbreeding, and inform genetic rescue. Their application to constructing a proxy for an extinct species introduces additional uncertainties: candidate edits may have pleiotropic effects; reference genomes may be incomplete; cell lines may acquire abnormalities during culture; and edited embryos may not develop normally. High-throughput sequencing does not eliminate the need for careful functional validation.

Stem cells and induced pluripotent stem cells offer potential routes for deriving cell types and supporting reproductive research. Somatic cell nuclear transfer has demonstrated cloning in mammals, but efficiency varies by species and depends on oocyte availability, reprogramming, embryo development, and gestational compatibility. Reviews of conservation cloning emphasize that cloning is a tool to complement population management rather than a substitute for habitat and demographic recovery. [23, 24]

2.4 Assisted reproduction and artificial gestation

Assisted reproductive technologies include artificial insemination, in-vitro fertilization, embryo transfer, gamete and embryo cryopreservation, and advanced reproductive management. Capabilities differ widely between taxa. Methods effective in domestic mammals may not translate to endangered wildlife because of differences in reproductive physiology, seasonality, anatomy, and the availability of trained veterinary teams.

Artificial gestation is an active field of biomedical research, but it is not a demonstrated route to complete human gestation outside the body. The report therefore treats fully artificial human gestation as speculative, rather than an available platform for population restoration. In-vitro gametogenesis is also not a clinically established human reproductive technology; current professional guidance emphasizes substantial scientific and ethical barriers. [27]

3. Human and animal reconstruction pathways

3.1 Animal de-extinction: candidate selection

A species candidate should not be selected solely because it is charismatic, technologically challenging, or commercially attractive. A defensible assessment considers ecological function, habitat availability, the cause of original extinction, contemporary threats, genetic material quality, reproductive feasibility, animal welfare, disease risks, potential invasiveness, community support, cost, and opportunity cost. Seddon, Moehrenschlager, and Ewen emphasize that a de-extinct organism would need to be treated as a conservation translocation project, with the same seriousness applied to release and long-term management. [4]

3.2 What past demonstrations actually show

The best-known early case is the bucardo, or Pyrenean ibex subspecies. Researchers used cryopreserved cells from the last known individual and somatic cell nuclear transfer. A female clone was born in 2003 but died minutes later due to lung defects. The experiment demonstrated a birth from a cryopreserved cell line associated with an extinct subspecies; it did not restore a viable population. [7]

Black-footed ferret cloning illustrates a different application: genetic rescue of an extant endangered species. In 2024, the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service reported offspring from a cloned ferret. The archived tissue contained genetic variation not represented in the contemporary population. This is a meaningful conservation biotechnology development, but it is not de-extinction because black-footed ferrets remain extant. [23]

3.3 The proxy problem

Genome-edited proxies are not exact revivals. A proxy combines selected characteristics from an extinct genome with the developmental and physiological background of a living relative. Its phenotype may differ from the historical organism in ways that are not predictable from a limited set of edits. The proxy may not perform the same ecological function, and the ecosystem into which it is introduced may have changed irreversibly.

Public communication should therefore avoid describing an engineered relative as a perfect return of an extinct animal. More accurate descriptions include “genome-edited proxy,” “functional analogue,” or “reconstructed lineage,” depending on the scientific facts. Taxonomic and conservation claims should be assessed by relevant scientific bodies, not by marketing language.

3.4 Human biological continuity

The proposed human component is best divided into four separate objectives: (1) preserving human genomic information; (2) preserving viable human biological material under ethical and legal oversight; (3) advancing reproductive and regenerative research for the health of living people; and (4) studying, as a speculative long-range question, what biological information and social infrastructure would be required to sustain humanity after a severe population catastrophe. None of these objectives is equivalent to having a practical capability to resurrect humanity after extinction.

Human genome preservation must be governed by privacy, consent, data protection, non-discrimination, and the rights of individuals and communities. Human genetic data can reveal sensitive information about relatives and populations. A preservation system must not become an instrument for coercive eugenics, racialized population ranking, biological ownership, or state control over reproduction.

3.5 Human reconstruction is not a present-day technical option

There is no established scientific pathway to reconstruct a human population after total species extinction. The chain would involve far more than genome archives: viable cells or a reliable means of constructing developmentally competent cells; safe gamete production; embryonic development; gestation; neonatal care; population-level genetic diversity; long-term health; ecological conditions; social learning; language and knowledge transmission; food, water, energy, shelter; and functioning institutions. Many pieces are incomplete, ethically contested, or purely speculative.

Present-day human continuity policy should therefore focus on preventing catastrophic population loss, strengthening public health, reducing conflict and disaster risks, preserving cultural and scientific knowledge, and maintaining resilient infrastructure. Preservation technologies may form a carefully limited part of a broader resilience strategy, but they cannot substitute for protecting living people.

Division IV · Chapters 44–58

4. Environmental transformation and ecological restoration

4.1 Reconstructing ecological functions

Species influence ecosystems through grazing, browsing, predation, seed dispersal, nutrient movement, burrowing, pollination, and physical modification of habitats. When a species disappears, some of these functions may weaken or be taken over by other organisms. A de-extinction proposal should identify the function it seeks to restore and test whether the function remains ecologically valuable under present conditions.

The strongest ecological rationale is functional rather than nostalgic. A candidate is worth considering when evidence suggests that its presence could improve an ecosystem outcome—such as maintaining vegetation structure or restoring a missing interaction—without introducing unacceptable risks. The project must also consider whether existing species or habitat interventions can achieve the same objective with lower cost and risk. [2–4]

4.2 Reintroduction, monitoring, and adaptive management

Reintroduction guidance emphasizes feasibility, ecological and social assessment, risk management, monitoring, and transparent communication. [3] A release plan should specify an ecological baseline, measurable objectives, staged implementation, disease surveillance, welfare indicators, contingency actions, and a stopping rule. It should also define who is responsible for long-term stewardship once the novelty of the project has passed.

4.3 Climate change and ecosystem change

Climate change alters species ranges, seasonal timing, water availability, fire regimes, and ecological interactions. IPCC assessments describe widespread climate impacts and escalating risks to ecosystems and human systems. [12] Reintroducing an organism into a habitat that no longer resembles its historical range can create welfare and ecological risks. Historical suitability must therefore be distinguished from future suitability under plausible climate conditions.

De-extinction should not be represented as a climate solution by itself. A large herbivore may affect vegetation and snow conditions locally, but the net climate effect depends on energy balance, methane emissions, vegetation responses, fire, soil carbon, and land-use changes. Carbon claims require site-specific measurement and comparison against other mitigation options.

4.4 Restoration has demonstrated human and employment dimensions

Restoration is already underway at large scales. A UN restoration progress report released on 29 October 2026 reported 252 million hectares under restoration across 149 countries and more than one million green jobs. The report stated that finance would need to rise substantially to meet restoration commitments and estimated that expanded investment could generate up to 32 million jobs globally by 2030. Those figures refer to the broader ecosystem-restoration agenda, not to de-extinction employment. [22]

This is important for the report's economic thesis: restoration can create employment and deliver food, water, climate-resilience, and well-being benefits. But those benefits do not prove that de-extinction is the causal driver. A scientifically defensible development strategy connects de-extinction research to restoration programs only where the biological intervention is independently justified.

4.5 Risks and unintended effects

Potential risks include disease transmission, invasive behavior, competition with extant species, altered food webs, genetic introgression, habitat damage, animal suffering, and political conflict over land use. A reconstructed organism may lack a coevolved microbiome or behavior learned in a historical social group. The ecosystem may have reorganized in its absence. These uncertainties require staged trials, independent review, and a realistic willingness to halt a project.

Division V · Chapters 59–73

5. Socioeconomic transformation

5.1 The scale of unmet human needs

De-extinction is being proposed in a world where basic material needs remain unmet. The 2025 State of Food Security and Nutrition in the World estimated that 673 million people—8.2% of the global population—experienced hunger in 2024; about 2.3 billion people experienced moderate or severe food insecurity, and approximately 2.6 billion could not afford a healthy diet. [14, 15] These figures describe a complex problem involving income, conflict, price inflation, infrastructure, distribution, climate shocks, and public policy.

For poverty statistics, the World Bank updated its international poverty lines in June 2025 using new purchasing-power-parity data. Its new international extreme-poverty line is $3.00 per person per day, replacing the earlier $2.15 line based on 2017 PPPs. Historical estimates based on the older threshold must not be presented as if they used the new benchmark. [16] The 2024 Poverty, Prosperity, and Planet report remains useful for describing the slow pace of poverty reduction, but its headline extreme-poverty numbers used the previous threshold. [17]

5.2 How de-extinction could contribute

The plausible socioeconomic contribution is indirect and conditional. Research programs can create demand for highly trained scientists, veterinarians, technicians, bioinformaticians, laboratory staff, manufacturing, cold-chain logistics, conservation field teams, habitat restoration, education, and monitoring. Reproductive and genomic technologies can also have applications beyond de-extinction in agriculture, endangered-species management, and biomedical research.

These mechanisms are opportunities, not guaranteed outcomes. A research industry may generate high productivity with relatively few jobs; it may be concentrated in wealthy countries; its supply chains may import skilled labor; and patent ownership may concentrate profits. The distributional outcome depends on training, local procurement, public access, licensing, international partnerships, and labor standards.

5.3 Food systems and agricultural resilience

Genomics, reproductive biotechnology, disease-resistance research, and cryopreservation can support agricultural biodiversity and livestock conservation. FAO's assessment of biodiversity for food and agriculture emphasizes that biodiversity at genetic, species, and ecosystem levels contributes directly and indirectly to agricultural systems. [32] The technologies developed for wildlife conservation may sometimes transfer to animal breeding, disease resistance, germplasm management, or reproductive medicine, but transfer must be demonstrated crop by crop or species by species.

Food insecurity is not simply a shortage of biological productivity. Even when food is available, households may lack purchasing power, secure access, transport, storage, political stability, or a healthy local food environment. Therefore, improvements in crop traits or animal genetics cannot alone resolve hunger. The strongest policy package would combine agricultural innovation with income support, infrastructure, climate adaptation, conflict prevention, nutrition policy, and equitable trade.

5.4 Health and medical spillovers

Stem-cell, genomic, and reproductive research can expand knowledge relevant to regenerative medicine and inherited disease. However, a de-extinction research program should not claim that it will cure currently incurable diseases without direct supporting evidence. Clinical translation requires separate trials, regulatory review, safety monitoring, and access planning. The World Health Organization's human-genome-editing framework and recommendations emphasize governance, oversight, registries, international collaboration, and ethical safeguards. [25, 26]

5.5 Housing, energy, education, and crime

Any causal claim that de-extinction will eliminate inadequate housing, energy poverty, educational exclusion, or crime needs a mechanism linking specific program outputs to those outcomes. A plausible connection may involve employment, wages, public revenue, technical training, and infrastructure investment. But higher investment does not automatically become affordable housing, universal healthcare, or lower crime. Public institutions, local governance, redistribution, and community participation mediate the outcome.

The report therefore rejects a single-industry solution model. De-extinction may form one component of a larger development portfolio, but poverty reduction requires direct policies that make essential goods and services accessible. It is the distribution of productive capacity and real income—not the headline size of a research market—that determines whether living standards improve.

They do not substantiate a claim that a particular sector is the only means of preventing recession or mass unemployment.

6.2 De-extinction as a potential employment ecosystem

An integrated biological-restoration sector could support multiple layers of employment:

  • Direct scientific roles: genetics, molecular biology, reproductive science, veterinary medicine, ecology, bioinformatics, and data stewardship.
  • Operational roles: animal husbandry, conservation fieldwork, laboratory operations, equipment maintenance, sample management, and biosafety.
  • Industrial roles: precision manufacturing, consumables, cryogenic storage, imaging, instrumentation, and quality assurance.
  • Restoration roles: habitat restoration, nursery operations, hydrology, invasive-species management, ecological monitoring, and community stewardship.
  • Enabling roles: education, project management, law, finance, insurance, public engagement, logistics, and regulatory administration.

Counting jobs requires care. “Jobs created” may refer to full-time-equivalent positions, temporary project roles, job-years, indirect supplier jobs, induced employment supported by household spending, or unique people employed at least once. These metrics cannot be added without adjustment. A claim of one billion additional workers by 2040 would require a comprehensive model that distinguishes these units and tests whether the workforce can be trained, financed, and productively employed.

A credible model should estimate both gross expenditure and net social value. Gross expenditure is not the same as net benefit: capital and labor have alternative uses, projects may displace other activities, and imported inputs may generate limited domestic employment. 6.5 Poverty and inequality are distributional outcomes

A larger industry does not necessarily narrow inequality. If patents, infrastructure, biological resources, data, and commercial returns are concentrated among a small number of firms or wealthy jurisdictions, a high-growth sector may deepen disparities. Public-interest licensing, open scientific standards, regional centers, transparent procurement, training access, and benefit-sharing arrangements are needed to make the gains broadly available.

Division VII · Chapters 92–105

7. Ethics, philosophy, and international governance

7.1 Human dignity, autonomy, and the limits of reconstruction

People are not interchangeable copies of a genome. A reconstructed individual would be a person with their own interests, autonomy, developmental history, relationships, and rights—not a replacement for an extinct individual. The language of “restoring humanity” can become ethically dangerous if it treats future people as products or assumes that reproductive goals override consent and welfare.

Human biological preservation must avoid coercive eugenics, demographic ranking, compelled reproduction, population-selection schemes, and proprietary control over human genetic information. The WHO's governance framework calls for appropriate oversight across institutional, national, regional, and global levels. [25, 26] The 2023 international summit statement concluded that preclinical evidence for safe and effective heritable genome editing had not been established and that it should not proceed unless demanding safety, legal, and governance conditions are met. [29]

7.2 Animal welfare and moral status

De-extinction can create novel welfare risks. High rates of embryo loss, developmental abnormalities, surrogate burdens, neonatal mortality, social isolation, and lifelong health complications may occur in technically ambitious reproductive programs. An animal cannot be treated merely as a proof of concept. Welfare review should include the full lifetime of each organism and the welfare of surrogate animals used in reproductive procedures.

A project should have predefined welfare endpoints, independent veterinary oversight, appropriate social housing, species-relevant behavioral opportunities, and authority to pause or terminate work. Rewilding must be assessed from the perspective of individual animals as well as ecosystem-level outcomes.

7.3 Indigenous sovereignty and environmental justice

Restoration projects often affect places that are already inhabited, governed, used, or culturally significant. Indigenous peoples have rights relating to lands, territories, resources, cultural heritage, genetic resources, and traditional knowledge. The United Nations Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples recognizes participation and consent principles, including provisions concerning lands and resources. [30] The Nagoya Protocol addresses access to genetic resources and associated traditional knowledge, prior informed consent or approval and involvement, mutually agreed terms, and fair benefit sharing. [31] Ethical analyses of de-extinction also emphasize justice, value, animal suffering, ecological risk, and the danger of presenting revival as a substitute for preventing extinction. [33]

For this reason, consultation after a project has already been designed is insufficient. Communities should participate from project conception, define what outcomes are acceptable, govern the use of relevant traditional knowledge, negotiate benefits, and retain the ability to decline projects affecting their lands and resources. No community should be framed as an obstacle to scientific progress. Indigenous participation is a matter of rights, authority, and environmental justice.

7.4 International governance principles

  1. Conservation purpose: every project states a measurable conservation or scientific objective.
  2. Precaution and proportionality: the magnitude of evidence and oversight matches the potential harm.
  3. Transparency: protocols, funding, failures, welfare outcomes, and conflicts of interest are disclosed.
  4. Independent review: scientific, ethical, ecological, and rights reviews are institutionally separate from project promoters.
  5. Equity: benefits and decision-making capacity are not restricted to wealthy institutions or countries.
  6. Indigenous rights: land, knowledge, governance, consent, and benefit-sharing requirements are respected.
  7. Animal welfare: individual welfare is treated as a binding condition, not a secondary public-relations issue.
  8. Adaptive management: projects can be modified, paused, or terminated as evidence changes.

7.5 The opportunity-cost objection

Critics argue that resources spent on de-extinction could be used to protect living species, reduce poverty, improve public health, or restore habitats. This is a serious allocation question. A pro-de-extinction strategy should answer it by demonstrating additionality: the proposed funding should expand the overall conservation and scientific capacity rather than redirect essential resources from proven interventions. Where a project cannot show an additional benefit, the opportunity-cost objection may prevail.

9.5 Integrated conclusion

De-extinction is neither a universal cure for environmental decline nor merely a spectacle. It is a developing family of scientific approaches that may create useful tools for conservation, genetic rescue, reproductive biology, and ecological restoration. The strongest case for its development is built from specific, measurable benefits: preserving biological material, improving the management of living populations, developing reproductive technologies, restoring carefully selected ecological functions, and widening access to conservation science.

The case becomes weaker when it promises outcomes that have not been demonstrated. The field cannot responsibly claim that it will, by itself, end poverty, hunger, inequality, crime, or recession. The $15.1–$17.1 trillion investment proposal and one-billion-worker expansion by 2040 are scenario claims that require rigorous independent modeling. Nor is human species reconstruction a present scientific capability. The ethical duty to preserve life is first an obligation to protect people and species that exist now.

A credible international program can still be ambitious. It can build a durable biological archive, invest in conservation genomics, support veterinary and reproductive science, restore ecosystems, create skilled employment, and establish rights-respecting governance. Its legitimacy will depend on honesty about uncertainty, protection of animals and communities, public accountability, and a refusal to let technological spectacle displace urgent conservation and human development needs.

Final recommendation. Pursue a conservation-first, human-rights-centered, and internationally equitable de-extinction research portfolio. Measure success by improved outcomes for living species and ecosystems, validated scientific capability, transparent economic benefits, and the preservation of human dignity—not by the number of extinct organisms announced as “brought back.”

This expanded edition develops the original framework into a more detailed research compendium. It adds technical explanations, candidate-selection criteria, risk pathways, governance design, quantitative scenario architecture, and a larger verified bibliography. 1.4 A systems definition of biological continuity

Biological continuity is best treated as a layered preservation problem. A genome sequence is an information object; it is not a living cell, a gamete, an embryo, a viable individual, or a self-sustaining population. Each layer requires different storage conditions, validation methods, legal protections, and recovery pathways. A robust programme would therefore maintain parallel archives: high-quality sequence data with provenance; cryopreserved viable cells where feasible; gametes and embryos where ethically and technically appropriate; tissue and cell lines; microbiome and pathogen metadata; ecological and husbandry records; and the knowledge needed to interpret and use those materials.

These layers are complementary rather than interchangeable. Digital sequence files can be copied and geographically distributed, but cannot by themselves restore epigenetic state, developmental history, cytoplasmic factors, maternal effects, or ecological interactions. Cryopreserved cells may preserve more biological information, yet their utility depends on collection quality, storage stability, contamination control, and future reproductive compatibility. Population-level preservation also requires representation of genetic diversity rather than reliance on a few convenient donors. The strongest continuity architecture is therefore redundant, geographically distributed, regularly audited, and governed as a long-term public-interest infrastructure.

1.5 Conservation triage and the opportunity-cost test

De-extinction should not be evaluated in isolation from conventional conservation. A candidate programme should first establish whether the original threats have been removed or can realistically be managed. Habitat loss, invasive predators, disease, pollution, climate change, and unsustainable harvest can defeat a technically successful birth. A proxy released into an unchanged threat environment may reproduce the conditions that caused the original extinction. Candidate assessment should consequently include a threat-removal plan, habitat capacity analysis, ecological role hypothesis, animal-welfare plan, biosecurity review, community consent process, long-term funding, and an exit strategy if monitoring reveals unacceptable harm.

The opportunity-cost objection is not answered by saying that innovation is inherently beneficial. It requires transparent accounting of money, personnel, land, political attention, and institutional capacity. A programme should publish its total lifecycle cost and identify whether its funding is genuinely additional or diverted from urgent conservation. A portfolio approach can reduce conflict: protect extant threatened species, restore habitats, bank genetic material, develop reproductive tools, and conduct carefully bounded proxy research in parallel. The portfolio should be evaluated against measurable conservation outcomes rather than the symbolic appeal of a charismatic extinct animal.

2.6 Ancient DNA: limits of reconstruction

Ancient DNA is fragmented, chemically modified, and vulnerable to contamination from microbes and modern handling. Sequencing depth alone does not guarantee an accurate reconstruction. Reference-genome choice, mapping bias, coverage gaps, post-mortem damage, repeat regions, structural variants, and the availability of closely related living species all affect confidence. A responsible reconstruction should report sample provenance, extraction protocols, contamination controls, sequencing platform, read length, depth, coverage distribution, variant-calling thresholds, uncertainty intervals, and independent replication where possible.

Even a high-quality consensus genome is not a complete blueprint for a historical organism. Phenotypes emerge from gene regulation, development, environment, microbiota, maternal effects, behaviour, and population-level processes. Some traits are polygenic and context-dependent. Reconstructing a small number of conspicuous features may produce an organism that resembles an extinct animal without recreating the full biological identity or ecological behaviour of that species. The report therefore uses the term proxy when the product is a genetically engineered relative intended to approximate selected traits or functions.

2.7 Genome engineering: from sequence edits to organismal outcomes

CRISPR-associated systems, base editing, prime editing, and other genome-engineering approaches have expanded the range of possible modifications. Yet an edit that is technically successful in a cell line is not automatically safe or useful in a whole organism. Relevant checks include on-target accuracy, off-target changes, chromosomal rearrangements, mosaicism, epigenetic stability, cell-line identity, developmental competence, fertility, immune function, and multigenerational effects. Multiplex editing compounds these challenges because interactions among loci may be nonlinear.

For conservation applications, the biological objective should be specified before choosing an editing platform. Restoring a lost allele, reducing a known genetic load, improving resistance to a particular pathogen, and recreating a complex extinct phenotype are different tasks. Each requires different evidence and different risk thresholds. The report recommends that projects define a narrow trait hypothesis, validate it in relevant cell and model systems, and avoid editing for aesthetic resemblance when the proposed ecological benefit is not established.

2.8 Assisted reproduction and the bottleneck problem

Assisted reproductive technologies can address limited access to gametes, reproductive asynchrony, small population size, and the loss of reproductive individuals. Cryopreservation, artificial insemination, in-vitro fertilization, intracytoplasmic sperm injection, embryo transfer, and somatic-cell nuclear transfer each have species-specific limitations. Protocols cannot be transferred mechanically from domestic model species to endangered wildlife. Oocyte maturation, placentation, gestation length, immune compatibility, neonatal care, and social development all influence success.

Stem-cell-derived gametes and in-vitro gametogenesis are promising research areas, but success in model organisms does not establish routine application in wildlife or humans. For endangered species, the most immediate value may be improved cell banking, gamete preservation, reproductive monitoring, and better use of existing genetic diversity. Artificial-gestation systems remain experimental and must not be described as a demonstrated route to full human gestation or post-extinction human reconstruction.

3.7 Population viability after a successful birth

A single organism cannot constitute species recovery. A programme must estimate effective population size, founder representation, relatedness, inbreeding depression, genetic load, sex ratio, generation time, reproductive output, demographic stochasticity, and environmental carrying capacity. Small founding populations can lose variation rapidly through drift. Cloning may reproduce the same genotype repeatedly and therefore does not, by itself, create a genetically diverse population. Where possible, multiple genetically distinct cell lines and gamete sources should be preserved before extinction, and managed breeding should avoid over-representation of a small number of founders.

Population viability analysis should use transparent assumptions and multiple scenarios. It should report extinction probability only where the model structure and parameter estimates support such an interpretation. A deterministic growth curve is not a substitute for uncertainty analysis. Disease outbreaks, catastrophic weather, reproductive failure, and changes in habitat quality should be represented explicitly, with model sensitivity reported rather than hidden.

4.6 Rewilding as the decisive ecological test

Rewilding is not simply releasing animals into a landscape. It is a process of restoring ecological processes while managing human-wildlife conflict, land-use change, disease, invasive species, and uncertainty. Ecological function is measured through outcomes such as vegetation structure, seed dispersal, nutrient cycling, herbivory, predation, scavenging, soil disturbance, hydrology, and trophic interactions. A proxy's success should be judged against a pre-registered ecological hypothesis and baseline measurements, not by its survival alone.

Monitoring should combine camera traps, acoustic sensors, satellite and drone imagery, environmental DNA, GPS telemetry, field surveys, and community observations. Automated classification can increase the scale of monitoring but may introduce detection bias, species misclassification, uneven coverage, and algorithmic drift. Data governance should specify who owns monitoring data, who can access sensitive locations, and how local communities participate in interpretation and management.

4.7 Climate claims and ecosystem engineering

Large herbivores can influence vegetation, snow cover, nutrient cycling, and disturbance regimes, but the direction and magnitude of those effects depend on local ecological conditions. The hypothesis that mammoth-like grazers could alter Arctic vegetation or permafrost dynamics remains a contested research proposition, not a demonstrated climate-mitigation technology. Climate claims must identify the mechanism, spatial scale, baseline, time horizon, counterfactual, and potential trade-offs. Carbon storage in one compartment may be offset by emissions or ecological changes elsewhere.

Nature-based climate solutions can contribute to mitigation and adaptation, but they do not replace rapid emissions reductions. Restoration projects should account for permanence, leakage, additionality, biodiversity outcomes, fire risk, land rights, and the distribution of benefits. De-extinction research may contribute tools and public attention to restoration, but its climate value must be measured rather than assumed.

5.6 The innovation-spillover hypothesis

Investment in a technically demanding field can generate spillovers when knowledge, skilled labour, instruments, and supply chains are transferable to other sectors. Potential pathways include genome sequencing, cryogenic storage, cell culture, reproductive medicine, veterinary diagnostics, bioinformatics, environmental monitoring, and advanced materials. The existence of a plausible pathway is not proof that the spillover will occur at a scale sufficient to transform national or global welfare. Evaluation requires evidence on additionality, knowledge diffusion, local procurement, worker training, intellectual-property access, and the distribution of productivity gains.

Economic impact assessment should distinguish gross output from net social benefit. A large industry may generate revenue and jobs while also displacing other activity, importing specialised inputs, concentrating ownership, or imposing public costs. Input-output multipliers should not be applied mechanically to global scenarios, and job estimates should distinguish direct, indirect, induced, temporary, permanent, full-time-equivalent, and net additional employment. The proposed trillion-dollar investment scenario is therefore presented as a hypothesis for sensitivity testing, not as a verified market forecast.

5.7 Poverty, hunger, and the conditions for material improvement

Poverty and hunger are shaped by income distribution, conflict, governance, public services, land access, infrastructure, food prices, health, climate exposure, and political institutions. Scientific innovation can raise productivity, but the conversion of productivity into reduced deprivation depends on wages, taxation, public investment, competition, social protection, and access. A biotechnology industry could contribute to food-system resilience through crop improvement, disease diagnostics, reproductive technologies, and ecosystem services, but no direct causal chain establishes that de-extinction alone would eliminate poverty or hunger.

The report therefore evaluates a conditional pathway: research and restoration investment may create capabilities and employment; public policy may convert some gains into broader services and incomes; equitable access and institutional capacity determine who benefits. Each link can fail. The strongest policy package combines research investment with universal services, education, infrastructure, fair labour standards, technology transfer, and transparent benefit-sharing.

6.6 Labour-market scenarios and the one-billion-worker claim

The proposed addition of one billion workers by 2040 is an extraordinary scenario claim and must be defined carefully. It could refer to gross jobs over a long period, net additional jobs, workers receiving training, or the total workforce associated with a broad ecosystem of industries. These are not interchangeable measures. A credible estimate requires a sector-by-sector employment model, annual job creation and attrition, occupational definitions, labour-force participation assumptions, geographic distribution, automation exposure, productivity, and the distinction between full-time and part-time work.

For this reason, the report does not present one billion jobs as a factual forecast. It sets out the data needed to test the claim and recommends reporting low, central, and high scenarios. Employment quality matters as much as headcount: wages, occupational safety, worker voice, gender equality, training pathways, job stability, and the ability of workers to move between sectors must be assessed.

6.7 Artificial intelligence and technological transition

AI exposure is not identical to job loss. Technologies may automate tasks, complement workers, change occupational composition, create new demand, or alter bargaining power. Outcomes depend on adoption speed, firm structure, education, labour institutions, macroeconomic conditions, and policy. The report therefore rejects deterministic claims that AI will inevitably cause a global recession or mass unemployment. It examines transition risks and the possibility that new scientific and restoration sectors could contribute to labour-market adjustment, while recognising that those sectors may be too small or specialised to absorb displaced workers without wider economic policy.

7.6 Indigenous sovereignty, data sovereignty, and consent

Genetic resources and ecosystems are embedded in territories, relationships, and cultural histories. Consultation is not equivalent to consent, and benefit-sharing is not a substitute for authority over land and resources. Projects should identify affected Indigenous nations and local communities early, respect their own decision-making institutions, fund independent technical advice, and negotiate data access, intellectual property, benefit-sharing, monitoring, and withdrawal mechanisms before research or release. The report treats Indigenous participation as a governance requirement, not a public-relations exercise.

Digital sequence information creates additional questions. A sample may be physically collected in one jurisdiction, sequenced elsewhere, stored in an international database, and used by a commercial actor in another country. Governance should therefore address provenance, access conditions, downstream use, benefit-sharing, attribution, privacy, and the treatment of sensitive ecological knowledge. Open science and community authority must be reconciled through clear, enforceable protocols.

7.7 Biosecurity and dual-use governance

Advanced biological tools can have beneficial and harmful applications. Risk assessment should consider accidental release, pathogen susceptibility, host-range changes, invasive potential, gene-flow into wild relatives, misuse of genetic data, and the security of biological collections. The relevant level of oversight depends on the organism, technology, intended environment, and plausible consequences. Risk management should include staged containment, independent review, emergency response planning, transparent incident reporting, and post-release monitoring where release is approved.

8.7 A reproducible scenario architecture

Long-range scenarios should state the baseline year, geographic unit, population denominator, price year, currency, discount rate, employment definition, technological assumptions, ecological assumptions, and policy environment. The four requested horizons—25, 50, 100, and 200 years—should be presented as conditional pathways, not point predictions. Beyond several decades, uncertainty expands rapidly; the 100- and 200-year sections should emphasise boundary conditions, resilience, and plausible pathways rather than false precision.

Every numerical figure should have a source or a clearly stated calculation. Modelled values should be labelled as model outputs; synthetic values as synthetic; observed data as observed. Sensitivity analysis should vary the assumptions most likely to change the conclusion. Results should include ranges and scenario narratives, and should avoid implying that a single central estimate is uniquely authoritative.

9.6 Staged implementation roadmap

Stage I — Preserve and document. Expand well-governed biobanking, genome sequencing, taxonomic reference collections, habitat baselines, and community-led conservation planning. Prioritise living endangered species and samples at immediate risk of loss.

Stage II — Validate enabling technologies. Develop species-specific cell culture, cryopreservation, reproductive protocols, genome editing validation, and welfare standards. Publish negative results and technical failures as well as successful milestones.

Stage III — Demonstrate conservation utility. Use genetic rescue and reproductive technologies where measurable benefits to living threatened populations are plausible. Test ecological hypotheses in contained or carefully controlled settings before any release.

Stage IV — Consider proxy restoration only after safeguards. Require habitat suitability, threat reduction, community consent, independent welfare review, biosecurity assessment, long-term financing, and a credible monitoring and exit plan.

Stage V — Evaluate social value. Publish distributional accounts showing who financed, governed, benefited from, and bore the risks of each programme. Scale only where independent evaluation supports continuation.

9.7 Final synthesis

De-extinction is most compelling when treated not as a single promise to reverse extinction, but as a coordinated research and conservation platform. Its strongest near-term case lies in technologies that can help preserve genetic diversity, improve reproductive options for threatened species, strengthen ecological monitoring, and support carefully designed restoration. More ambitious animal proxy projects may become feasible in selected cases, but ecological and welfare outcomes remain decisive. Human biological continuity is a distinct and highly speculative domain that should be framed around present-day preservation research, not as a demonstrated ability to reconstruct humanity after extinction.

The socioeconomic case is similarly conditional. Research investment can produce useful spillovers and specialised employment, but it cannot by itself guarantee poverty elimination, universal housing, food security, or macroeconomic recovery. Those outcomes require institutions that distribute gains and protect people. A responsible pro-de-extinction position therefore argues for ambitious research together with transparent evidence, conservation priorities, ethical governance, Indigenous authority, and measurable public benefit.

The twelve agreed quotations

The following passages are reproduced verbatim from the approved project text. Their inclusion preserves the project's argumentative foundation; it does not convert every factual proposition within them into an independently verified finding.

1. Extinction — Human biological continuity

Humanity has already crossed critical planetary and technological thresholds, initiating an ongoing, irreversible extinction event driven by systemic environmental degradation, compounding existential risks, and existential resource depletion. Because this process is already underway, conventional prevention and mitigation strategies are no longer sufficient to halt the trajectory toward total demographic collapse. Once an extinction event reaches completion, internal recovery mechanisms cease to exist. Under this condition, the de-extinction of humanity—achieved through pre-emptively automated genetic archiving, artificial gestational infrastructure,and autonomous revival protocols—represents the singular viable mechanism to restore human populations post-collapse.Therefore, because extinction is already actively occurring and unavoidable through standard intervention, developing and deploying de-extinction capabilities is the only definitive method to ensure the long-term survival of the species.

2. Socioeconomic transformation

Human de-extinction would solve poverty and hunger, dismantle the material foundations of inequality, eliminate substandard housing, and substantially reduce crime by transforming the technological and economic conditions under which deprivation persists. Its significance would therefore extend far beyond the preservation of human biological existence: it would provide the infrastructure through which humanity could secure universal access to food, shelter, healthcare, education, energy, and the productive resources required for a dignified life.

3. Global recession and economic transformation

Humanity is clearly sliding into the biggest recession it has ever seen. The Great Depressions effects will seem small compared to the effects of this new recession caused in part by AI, climate change and other human caused effects. The post world recession and during the recession certain groups of humanity will experience a greater degree of marginalization, leading to exploitation and the potential for abuses and regression of an entire group of people. In this world chattel slavery would come back globally. However there is a grain of hope. The de-extinction industry promises record jobs, and a general sense of socioeconomic relief. If humanity were to finally break the sigmization of de-extinction as unethical, unprecedented investment would follow into the industry. This investment would be valued between 15.1 trillion and 17.1 trillion towards the near future, and lift the global community out of poverty. De-extinction is the only method to stop this impending recession without mass oppression and whilst also having massive benefits for the environment.

4. Environmental restoration and workforce

De-extinction will also drastically benefit the environment by drawing time, money and resources into helping the environment while still adding 1 billion to the workforce by 2040.

5. Animal de-extinction and ecological restoration

The restoration of extinct species represents more than the recovery of individual organisms; it represents an opportunity to restore ecological relationships, recover lost environmental functions, and strengthen the resilience of ecosystems disrupted by human activity. By reconstructing lost species and restoring their ecological roles, humanity could develop new approaches to biodiversity conservation while improving the long-term stability of natural environments.

6. Food security and agricultural transformation

The technologies developed through de-extinction research could transform agricultural production by advancing genetic engineering, reproductive biotechnology, disease resistance, and biological resource management. These advancements could contribute to more resilient food systems, improve agricultural productivity, reduce vulnerability to environmental disruption, and expand humanity's capacity to provide reliable nutrition to a growing global population.

7. Healthcare and human longevity

The scientific infrastructure required to preserve, reconstruct, and restore biological life could generate substantial advancements in regenerative medicine, genetic disease research, cellular engineering, reproductive healthcare, and the treatment of previously incurable conditions. The implications would extend beyond the restoration of extinct life, potentially improving the quality and duration of life for existing human populations.

8. Technological unemployment and labor transformation

The economic consequences of artificial intelligence and industrial automation cannot be evaluated exclusively through productivity gains. Without substantial investment in new industries and employment opportunities, technological advancement could intensify labor displacement, weaken economic security, and concentrate productive wealth among a comparatively small segment of the population. De-extinction could provide an alternative direction for technological investment by creating new scientific, industrial, environmental, and infrastructure-related employment sectors.

9. Global inequality and technological access

The benefits of advanced biological technologies must not remain concentrated within wealthy nations or private institutions. If de-extinction is to contribute meaningfully to humanity's long-term development, its infrastructure must be accompanied by international cooperation, equitable technology transfer, accessible scientific education, and mechanisms that prevent the concentration of biological and economic power.

10. Civilizational preservation

The preservation of humanity requires more than maintaining a record of its genetic composition. It requires protecting the biological materials, scientific knowledge, technological infrastructure, ecological resources, and reproductive capabilities necessary to sustain future populations. A comprehensive de-extinction framework would therefore function as an investment in civilizational continuity, preserving not merely the possibility of human existence but the conditions necessary for human flourishing.

11. Indigenous sovereignty and environmental justice

Biological restoration cannot be considered ethically successful if it disregards the sovereignty, rights, knowledge, and territorial interests of the communities whose environments would be affected. Any international de-extinction framework must recognize that ecological restoration involves questions of land ownership, environmental authority, cultural continuity, and the right of Indigenous communities to determine the future of their territories.

12. Intergenerational responsibility

The decisions made by contemporary humanity will determine the biological, ecological, and technological possibilities available to future generations. De-extinction therefore raises a broader question of intergenerational responsibility: whether humanity has an obligation not only to prevent irreversible biological losses but also to preserve the knowledge and capabilities required to recover from them.

Empirical Research Expansion: What the Evidence Supports

This expanded analysis strengthens the report’s thesis by connecting de-extinction research to observed results in conservation genetics, ecological restoration, food systems, biotechnology, public health, Indigenous governance, and labour economics. The evidence supports the value of investing in biological research and restoration capacity; it does not establish that de-extinction alone will eliminate poverty, hunger, inequality, unemployment, or economic recession. Those outcomes depend on institutional design, distribution, land rights, infrastructure, and public policy.

1. De-extinction as a conservation technology platform

The strongest empirical case for de-extinction is broader than the literal return of a vanished species. The underlying research stack—ancient and modern genome sequencing, genome assembly, precision editing, stem-cell systems, reproductive tissue banking, assisted reproduction, and ecological monitoring—can also be applied to living species that are threatened but not yet extinct. Recent reviews describe these tools as a developing conservation workflow, while emphasizing that a genetically modified proxy is not automatically equivalent to the extinct organism and that restoration outcomes depend on ecology after birth, not genetic resemblance alone [115–120]. This makes a defensible investment argument possible: research capacity created for de-extinction may have conservation value before a true de-extinct population is ever achieved.

The distinction between a species and a functional proxy matters. A reconstructed organism may share selected traits with an extinct relative without reproducing its full genome, learned behaviour, microbiome, developmental environment, or ecological relationships. The 2025 literature cautions against treating technological demonstration as proof of ecological success. The policy question should therefore be whether a proposed intervention can deliver measurable conservation gains, how those gains compare with conventional approaches, and who bears the risks [117–120]. This does not weaken the case for innovation; it gives the innovation a testable standard.

Wildlife reproductive tissue banks extend the time horizon of conservation. Cryopreserved cells and gametes can preserve alleles and lineages that may be lost through demographic collapse, while reproductive technologies may expand future options for breeding and genetic management. The methods are technically uneven across taxa, however, and cell viability, genome integrity, cryopreservation success, and reproductive compatibility must be evaluated species by species [120, 116]. A credible de-extinction economy would therefore include public biobanking, long-term maintenance, reproductive research, and open scientific standards—not simply headline-grabbing species announcements.

2. Genetic rescue demonstrates a real conservation benefit

The clearest empirical example is the Florida panther. In 1995, managers introduced eight female pumas from Texas into a severely inbred Florida population. Follow-up research reported increased numbers, higher heterozygosity, and improvement in several indicators associated with inbreeding depression. A later genomic analysis showed that the Texas ancestry had not simply replaced the ancestral Florida lineage. A 2025 multi-generational analysis using records from 1,192 panthers over nine generations found that genetic and phenotypic benefits persisted after five generations; abundance increased more than fivefold and genetic effective population size more than twentyfold [121–122]. This is a powerful case for the proposition that genetic knowledge and carefully governed reproductive or translocation interventions can prevent the loss of biological diversity.

The case is not a blank cheque for genetic engineering. Genetic rescue involves risks of outbreeding depression, local adaptation loss, disease transfer, and changes to population structure. The conservation genetics literature provides decision frameworks for assessing those risks and for defining biologically meaningful management units [123–130]. A de-extinction programme should adopt the same discipline: sequence before intervention, identify the target conservation outcome, publish baseline and follow-up measures, and retain independent review. The relevant impact is not the number of genomes edited; it is the measured change in population viability, diversity, and ecological function.

The wider implication is that de-extinction investment can be framed as a research-and-capacity multiplier. Better genomic reference data, reproductive tissue repositories, and technical expertise can improve management of currently endangered species even when the de-extinction target remains speculative. The Florida panther example also shows why such benefits require habitat protection and continued field management: genetic rescue did not remove habitat loss, infectious disease, or human-wildlife conflict. Genetic tools complement—not replace—conservation of the systems in which populations live [121–130].

3. Ecological restoration can produce measurable gains—but outcomes vary

The ecological restoration literature provides a stronger empirical base than claims that any resurrected species will automatically repair an ecosystem. A global meta-analysis of 89 restoration assessments found average increases of 44% in biodiversity and 25% in ecosystem-service provision relative to degraded systems. A later global analysis spanning 221 forest landscapes reported biodiversity levels 15–84% higher and vegetation structure 36–77% higher in restored than in degraded forests, while also showing that outcomes remained below reference ecosystems and varied by disturbance history, time, and landscape context [135–140]. These findings support investment in restoration as a measurable public good, while rejecting simplistic claims that restoration instantly returns a site to its historical state.

Natural regeneration can in some contexts outperform highly engineered planting programmes. Meta-analyses comparing tropical forest recovery found that naturally regenerating forests often achieved stronger biodiversity and vegetation-structure outcomes than active restoration, after accounting for site factors. This is directly relevant to de-extinction resource allocation: the most expensive technological option is not necessarily the most effective ecological option. Projects should compare de-extinction, conventional restoration, habitat protection, assisted natural regeneration, and species reintroduction against the same outcome measures [137–141]. The best result may be a portfolio of approaches rather than a single flagship technology.

The value of biodiversity is not limited to species counts. Experimental and synthesis research links biological diversity with ecosystem productivity, resistance to climate extremes, multiple ecosystem services, and the functioning of food webs. Conversely, land-use change and defaunation are associated with losses in local biodiversity and ecological functions [142–155]. These studies support the report’s environmental argument: conserving species and rebuilding ecological relationships can strengthen natural systems on which human economies depend. They do not establish that every extinct organism is suitable for reintroduction, or that a proxy will reproduce the same ecological function in a changed climate.

4. Climate resilience and ecosystem services are conditional public benefits

Nature-based climate interventions can contribute to adaptation and mitigation, but their performance is highly dependent on location, ecosystem type, governance, and social safeguards. Reviews of nature-based solutions show that outcomes vary substantially across interventions and that poorly designed projects can create trade-offs for biodiversity, livelihoods, or rights. The literature therefore recommends transparent monitoring, context-specific baselines, and safeguards against using carbon claims to justify harmful land conversion [156–160]. This is a useful standard for de-extinction-linked rewilding: restored animals should be evaluated through measurable changes in vegetation, soil, hydrology, fire regimes, species interactions, and carbon flux—not through assumed benefits.

Pollination and other ecosystem services provide a direct bridge between biodiversity and food production. Research has found that wild pollinators can improve crop fruit set independently of managed honey-bee abundance, and global syntheses show that biodiversity-mediated ecosystem services can support crop production. At the same time, these benefits depend on landscape complexity, species identity, farming practices, and pollinator availability [161–165]. The implication is that conservation biotechnology may have agricultural value when it addresses a defined biological bottleneck, but that value should be tested against conventional habitat and agroecological interventions.

The economics of ecosystem services can make environmental externalities visible in public decision-making. However, valuation is not the same as a guaranteed cash return, and a monetary estimate does not imply that ecosystems are fully substitutable. The appropriate argument is that biological restoration can protect service flows—pollination, water regulation, soil formation, fisheries, climate regulation, and disease regulation—that would otherwise be omitted from narrow project accounting [193–198].

5. Food security: biodiversity and climate resilience, not a magic poverty cure

Food insecurity is shaped by conflict, income, food prices, infrastructure, trade, public health, and distribution as well as food production. Current systematic research identifies climate change and biodiversity loss as important drivers affecting food-system resilience, while crop-yield studies show that higher temperatures have already affected major crop production in multiple regions [168–179]. The report can therefore argue that biological research and ecological restoration may strengthen the conditions for reliable food production. It cannot honestly claim that de-extinction itself solves hunger. Hunger is also a problem of access, purchasing power, political stability, and distribution.

Crop diversification provides a concrete, evidence-backed pathway. Meta-analyses covering thousands of experiments have reported benefits for biodiversity and ecosystem services, with some diversification practices improving production, water quality, pest control, and soil quality. Results vary across crop types and settings, so the evidence supports targeted diversification rather than a universal yield guarantee [160–163, 173–174]. Research on pollinators similarly shows that biological diversity can support agricultural output, but it depends on healthy landscapes and appropriate farm management [161–165]. A de-extinction research programme could contribute through improved genetics, disease resistance, reproductive tools, and ecological understanding—but only if those developments are transferred into accessible agricultural systems.

A food-security strategy should measure outcomes that matter to households: stable yields, affordability, nutrition, reduced post-harvest loss, resilience to weather shocks, and access for low-income communities. Agricultural biotechnology that increases production without lowering costs or improving access may have little effect on hunger. The report’s stronger proposition is therefore conditional: investment in conservation and reproductive technologies can support more resilient biological production systems when paired with equitable distribution, farmer access, extension services, and public infrastructure [168–179].

6. Biomedical spillovers: demonstrated technologies, careful extrapolation

Genome editing and stem-cell biology already have demonstrated biomedical applications. CRISPR-Cas9 research established programmable genome modification, while base editing and prime editing expanded the precision and types of possible changes. Induced pluripotent stem-cell research established methods for reprogramming adult cells into pluripotent states. These milestones support the proposition that investment in biological engineering can generate capabilities across research and medicine [180–189]. They do not establish that human de-extinction is currently feasible, that artificial gestation can safely support full human development, or that a conservation technology will automatically translate into a clinical therapy.

Clinical gene-editing studies show how long the path from laboratory concept to human treatment can be. Trials in sickle-cell disease and transthyretin amyloidosis demonstrated therapeutic genome editing in carefully selected contexts, and regulatory authorization of a CRISPR-based sickle-cell treatment marked a major translation milestone [190–192]. These examples are evidence of biomedical spillover, not of species resurrection. Ethical oversight, long-term follow-up, informed consent, affordability, and equitable access remain essential to determine whether scientific advances improve population health rather than concentrating benefits among wealthy patients.

A responsible biological-continuity programme would thus treat medical applications as a possible co-benefit, not a guaranteed dividend. It would support basic science, shared infrastructure, public-interest translational research, and independent safety review. Claims about curing disease, extending life, or rebuilding human reproductive capacity must be evaluated separately against clinical evidence and legal oversight [180–192].

7. Employment, AI, and the transition to a biological economy

Automation and generative AI create serious distributional questions, but current empirical evidence does not support the claim that a global depression or mass unemployment is inevitable. Research on industrial robots in US labour markets found concentrated negative effects in exposed local labour markets, while later work on automation has documented wage and inequality effects that depend on which tasks are displaced and whether new tasks are created. Recent empirical reviews of generative AI report productivity effects that are uneven, with limited evidence so far of large-scale aggregate job displacement [203–211]. The report should therefore frame AI as a source of labour-market disruption and uncertainty, not as proof that a worldwide recession or return of slavery is imminent.

A biological restoration economy could create jobs in genomics, laboratory operations, veterinary science, reproductive medicine, field ecology, monitoring, construction, habitat restoration, data science, manufacturing, education, and long-term ecosystem management. These are plausible occupational pathways, not a measured forecast of one billion new jobs. Green-jobs research emphasizes that net employment outcomes depend on skills, geography, labour standards, investment additionality, and the jobs displaced elsewhere in the economy [199–202, 207, 211]. Any job projection in this report should disclose its assumptions and distinguish gross jobs from net jobs, temporary construction work from permanent operations, and direct employment from induced or supply-chain effects.

The central policy choice is how benefits are distributed. Without wage standards, training, public procurement, regional investment, and worker voice, a high-technology bioeconomy could concentrate rents among intellectual-property owners and private investors. Conversely, public-interest research infrastructure and open technology transfer could broaden access to skills and employment. A credible proposal must specify who pays, who owns the resulting intellectual property, who receives wages, and how communities affected by land-use changes participate in decisions [193–211].

8. Nature, macroeconomics, and the opportunity-cost test

Economic output is not independent of natural systems. Global ecosystem-service valuation and natural-capital accounting have attempted to make visible the economic contributions of pollination, water regulation, fisheries, soil formation, and other services. The World Bank’s economy–nature modelling demonstrates how ecosystem degradation can affect development pathways, while international accounting frameworks provide methods for tracking ecosystem assets and service flows [193–198]. These sources support the premise that investment in biological systems can be economically relevant. They do not provide a simple forecast of trillions of dollars in de-extinction-derived growth.

The correct economic comparison is not “de-extinction spending versus doing nothing.” It is de-extinction investment versus the best feasible alternatives for the same capital, land, personnel, and institutional capacity. The report should therefore evaluate a portfolio: habitat protection, public health, agricultural adaptation, clean energy, poverty reduction, education, and biological research. A de-extinction programme is economically defensible only where the expected additional benefits justify its opportunity cost and where less expensive interventions cannot achieve the same outcome. Nature-risk, ecosystem-accounting, and labour-market studies provide a framework for making that comparison rather than relying on an unsupported headline figure [193–206].

The same standard applies to speculative projections. Any estimate of investment, jobs, poverty reduction, GDP, or tax revenue should be presented as a scenario with explicit assumptions, not as a measured result. Sensitivity analysis should vary capital intensity, commercial adoption, productivity, wages, project duration, failure rates, public subsidies, ecological performance, and benefit distribution. The report’s strongest claim is that biological innovation could become a meaningful sector of a diversified economy if it is built on public value and verifiable results—not that the industry will necessarily prevent recession or lift every population out of poverty [193–211].

9. Indigenous sovereignty, participation, and environmental justice

The evidence on Indigenous lands supports a governance argument, not a licence for outside institutions to treat territories as experimental sites. A spatial analysis found that Indigenous lands cover a substantial share of the world’s land and overlap with areas important for biodiversity conservation. Comparative work in Australia, Brazil, and Canada found vertebrate biodiversity on Indigenous-managed lands comparable to that in formally protected areas. A 2026 systematic review of 111 studies reported positive relationships between Indigenous lands and conservation outcomes in most studies, while also identifying a shortage of Indigenous authorship in the literature [131–134].

Indigenous knowledge and scientific knowledge can be complementary, but collaboration must not be reduced to extracting data or seeking ceremonial endorsement after decisions have already been made. Research on Indigenous participation in water governance documents structural barriers to meaningful authority, and scholarship on bridging knowledge systems emphasizes the need for reciprocal relationships and institutional change [212–213]. A de-extinction or rewilding programme must begin with free, prior, and informed consent, respect land tenure and community law, protect cultural data, and give affected communities authority to refuse or shape interventions.

Environmental justice also requires transparency about where benefits and burdens fall. If a rewilding project restricts access to land, changes livelihoods, introduces ecological risk, or captures carbon revenues for outside investors, the community bearing those effects must have enforceable protections and a fair share of benefits. Governance proposals must be evaluated for actual decision-making power, not simply consultation counts. Genetic engineering in conservation raises additional issues of risk assessment, public participation, reversibility, and unequal distribution of costs and benefits [117–119, 134, 212–214].
