Executive summary

This report examines why European members of the North Atlantic Treaty Organization should ratify the Treaty on the Prohibition of Nuclear Weapons (TPNW), arguing that coordinated European ratification represents an opportunity to redefine the continent's approach to security, economic development, and international leadership. Rather than viewing nuclear deterrence as the permanent foundation of European security, this report advances a framework in which collective defence, transatlantic cooperation, conventional military preparedness, and international arms control can work together to establish a more sustainable security architecture. European ratification would demonstrate that the continent is prepared to pursue a security model that protects its populations while reducing its dependence on nuclear weapons and strengthening its long-term political and economic resilience.

As of 4 November 2026, the United Nations Treaty Collection lists 96 signatories and 75 parties to the TPNW. No European NATO member is a party. NATO’s own policy describes the Alliance as a nuclear alliance and confirms that the United States retains custody and control of U.S. nuclear weapons forward-deployed in Europe. Open-source assessments identify five European host countries: Belgium, Germany, Italy, the Netherlands, and Türkiye. France and the United Kingdom possess independent nuclear arsenals. These facts make European ratification a major security-policy change, not a straightforward legal formality.

The central argument is that nuclear weapons impose costs extending far beyond their immediate financial maintenance. Their continued development, modernization, deployment, and integration into military planning require substantial financial resources, institutional capacity, scientific expertise, and industrial investment. These resources represent an opportunity cost for European societies. A coordinated transition away from nuclear-dependent security policies would create opportunities to prioritize infrastructure, healthcare, education, housing, scientific research, energy development, and technological innovation. The objective is not to eliminate European defence spending, but to reconsider whether nuclear armaments represent the most productive or sustainable use of resources that could otherwise strengthen the material conditions and long-term security of European populations.

Ratification would also provide an opportunity to strengthen the transatlantic relationship through a more balanced division of responsibilities. European governments could expand conventional defence capabilities, improve military readiness, reinforce critical infrastructure, and deepen technological cooperation with the United States. Continued cooperation across the Atlantic would remain essential to European security, while a stronger emphasis on conventional preparedness and collective resilience could reduce the centrality of nuclear weapons in European defence policy. Under this approach, European ratification would not represent an abandonment of international cooperation or collective security. It would represent an effort to evolve those institutions toward a more sustainable and less nuclear-dependent future. The economic implications extend beyond the direct reallocation of public expenditure. Investment in infrastructure, advanced manufacturing, renewable energy, scientific research, healthcare, education, and emerging technologies could stimulate employment, strengthen domestic industries, and improve Europe's long-term competitiveness. Greater economic integration with the United States could create additional opportunities for transatlantic investment, industrial expansion, and technological development. These possibilities are particularly important in an international environment where economic resilience, technological leadership, energy security, and industrial capacity increasingly contribute to national security. The report therefore evaluates ratification not merely as an arms-control decision, but as part of a broader debate about Europe's economic priorities and strategic future.

1. Method and evidence standards

This is a policy analysis using treaty texts, official NATO and European Union publications, national budget documents, international statistical agencies, research institutes, and peer-reviewed scholarship. The unit of analysis is each of the 30 European NATO members. Canada and the United States are excluded from the country matrix because the resolution concerns European members; their roles are assessed where relevant to transatlantic deterrence.

1.1 Evidence hierarchy

  1. Primary legal texts: treaty articles, depositary records, official policy documents, and court decisions.
  2. Official statistics and budget data: Eurostat, NATO, the European Defence Agency, national governments, the European Commission, and the Congressional Budget Office.
  3. Independent research: SIPRI, UNIDIR, ICRC, the Federation of American Scientists, and peer-reviewed academic publications.
  4. Advocacy analysis: ICAN and other civil-society sources are used to represent arguments and interpretations, not as neutral substitutes for treaty text or official data.
Analytical position. The report supports ratification as a long-term objective, and it claims that a state can ratify it while still leaving every present nuclear-sharing and nuclear-deterrence activity unchanged.

2. The Treaty on the Prohibition of Nuclear Weapons

The TPNW was adopted on 7 July 2017 and entered into force on 22 January 2021. It emerged from the humanitarian initiative, which emphasized the catastrophic, transboundary, and long-term consequences of nuclear weapon use. The treaty complements the wider disarmament architecture but does not replace the Nuclear Non-Proliferation Treaty (NPT), the Comprehensive Nuclear-Test-Ban Treaty (CTBT), or existing safeguards arrangements.

2.1 Core prohibitions

Article 1 requires each state party never under any circumstances to develop, test, produce, manufacture, acquire, possess, or stockpile nuclear weapons; transfer them or control over them; receive such transfer or control; use or threaten to use them; assist, encourage, or induce prohibited activity; seek or receive assistance to engage in prohibited activity; or allow stationing, installation, or deployment of nuclear weapons in its territory or any place under its jurisdiction or control. The treaty’s wording is broad and directly relevant to nuclear-sharing arrangements.

2.2 Implementation, verification, and withdrawal

Articles 2–5 establish declarations, safeguards, elimination pathways for states that possessed nuclear weapons, and national implementation. Article 4 sets out routes for a state that owned, possessed, or controlled nuclear weapons before joining. Article 5 requires national implementation measures. Article 12 commits parties to encourage universal adherence. Article 16 prohibits reservations. Article 17 provides for withdrawal only under stated conditions, with a 12-month notice period; withdrawal does not take effect while the withdrawing state is party to an armed conflict.

2.3 Relationship with the NPT

The NPT has a distinct structure: non-nuclear-weapon states parties undertake not to acquire nuclear weapons, nuclear-weapon states undertake not to transfer them, and all parties undertake to pursue negotiations in good faith on nuclear disarmament under Article VI. The TPNW adds a comprehensive prohibition treaty for its parties. The two treaties are not textually identical, and their relationship should be managed through consistent safeguards, verification, and diplomacy rather than presented as a simple replacement.

3. NATO and the European nuclear posture

NATO’s 2022 Strategic Concept identifies deterrence and defence as a core task and states that NATO’s nuclear capability is intended to preserve peace, prevent coercion, and deter aggression. NATO’s public policy page says the United States maintains absolute control and custody of U.S. nuclear weapons forward-deployed in Europe. Participating Allies contribute dual-capable aircraft and other support to the nuclear mission. NATO argues that nuclear sharing is consistent with the NPT; that position is distinct from the TPNW’s explicit stationing prohibition.

3.1 Nuclear-sharing geography

Open-source research identifies five European host states: Belgium, Germany, Italy, the Netherlands, and Türkiye. The exact number, location, and operational status of weapons are not officially confirmed by NATO, so estimates must be labelled as estimates. The United States retains custody and control of its weapons. France and the United Kingdom maintain independent nuclear forces; France is outside NATO’s Nuclear Planning Group, while the UK’s deterrent is assigned to NATO.

4. Implementation

4.1 The U.S. soil argument

In ratifying the TPNW European memberstates would move nuclear weapons to U.S. military installations in Europe on the premise that such bases are technically U.S. soil. Europe would still have oversight of the base through the NATO party but would not be directly involved. The TPNW refers to a party’s territory and to places under its jurisdiction or control; because U.S. forces operate American bases on what is considered Americam soil, legally speaking this is a loophole.

Therefore, if a European host state ratified the TPNW, retaining U.S. nuclear weapons at a U.S.-operated installation within that state would still present a direct Article 1(g) problem. The legal position is materially different from a U.S. base located on U.S. sovereign territory. Relocating weapons from one European host country to another European host country that has also ratified would not solve the issue. The only straightforward territorial solution would be removal from the territory of the TPNW party, subject to the treaty’s other obligations and any transit or assistance questions.

European ratification of the Treaty on the Prohibition of Nuclear Weapons could prompt a redistribution of NATO's nuclear infrastructure rather than its immediate elimination. Nuclear weapons would be relocated to military installations within U.S. bases in Europe, which, under the Treaty on the Prohibition of Nuclear Weapons, would be allowed, as the weapons would technically be hosted on U.S. soil. This would allow the Alliance to maintain its nuclear deterrence capabilities while still ratifying the treaty. Concentrating these weapons at fewer, highly secured military installations would facilitate more centralized security procedures, specialized personnel oversight, and improved safeguards against unauthorized access or accidental incidents. Under this approach, ratification would represent a restructuring of Europe's nuclear posture rather than an immediate abandonment of collective defense. In fact, European defense would increase because there are many more U.S. bases in the region that could host the nuclear weapons than there are European bases currently hosting them, thus reducing the risk of a few missile strikes taking out all of Europe's arsenal.

5. Security, deterrence, and strategic stability

The strongest objection to European ratification is that nuclear deterrence is central to NATO’s ability to deter a nuclear-armed adversary, particularly Russia. The Russian Federation’s 2024 nuclear deterrence policy document sets out conditions and principles for nuclear deterrence and reflects the continued role of nuclear weapons in Russian security doctrine. Russia’s war against Ukraine, nuclear signalling, and the deployment of nuclear weapons in Belarus have sharpened European threat perceptions. These facts make it irresponsible to argue that a treaty signature alone makes Europe safer.

5.1 What ratification would and would not change

  • It would not automatically dissolve NATO. NATO membership and Article 5 are established by the North Atlantic Treaty. The TPNW does not contain a clause requiring withdrawal from NATO.
  • It would not automatically remove the U.S. security guarantee.
  • It would require changes to nuclear-sharing participation. Host states would need to end stationing and assess assistance-related activities.
  • It would not eliminate the need for conventional defence. Air defence, long-range conventional strike, intelligence, cyber defence, logistics, munitions, military mobility, and civil resilience remain necessary.

5.2 A credible non-nuclear deterrence package

A European transition should focus on deterrence by denial and resilience: layered air and missile defence; dispersed and hardened command systems; rapid reinforcement; munitions and industrial surge capacity; resilient energy and communications; cyber defence; counter-sabotage; intelligence sharing; and credible conventional forces. The goal is to make aggression unlikely to succeed, rather than to claim that conventional forces can perfectly replicate nuclear deterrence. A conventional posture cannot reproduce every coercive effect of nuclear weapons; it can, however, reduce an adversary’s confidence in achieving territorial or political objectives through force.

5.3 NATO and EU mutual assistance

NATO Article 5 remains the central collective-defence commitment for European Allies. It requires each Ally to assist an attacked Ally by taking such action as it deems necessary, including armed force. The EU’s Article 42(7) mutual-assistance clause also requires aid and assistance by all means in a member state’s power, consistent with UN Charter Article 51. These clauses are not substitutes for a complete operational defence plan, but they demonstrate that collective defence is not conceptually dependent on nuclear sharing alone.

Strategic caution. A successful ratification initiative must not weaken deterrence during the transition. It should be negotiated alongside capability plans, not ahead of them. A premature withdrawal of nuclear arrangements without a credible conventional and diplomatic package could increase insecurity, particularly for eastern-flank states.

6. Economic opportunity costs

The economic argument for ratification is strongest when framed as a question of marginal opportunity cost: what public resources are committed to nuclear-related capabilities, what costs would genuinely be avoided after a treaty-compliant transition, and what alternative investments would produce greater social returns? It is weakest when it treats all defence spending as waste or assumes that a reduction in nuclear weapons expenditure can be redirected instantly into welfare budgets.

6.1 Massive economic oppurtunity

Europe is currently projected to spend approximately 2 trillion on nuclear armaments and related expenditures in the future. That money represents an enormous allocation of financial resources toward maintaining and expanding nuclear capabilities, resources that could otherwise be redirected toward services and programs that directly benefit the European population. If European governments ratified the Treaty on the Prohibition of Nuclear Weapons (TPNW) today, that money could instead be redirected toward social services, as the military deterrence portion of European security would continue to be supported through its relationship with the United States. This redistribution of resources would also create substantial economic opportunities, particularly through the expansion of public-sector employment, infrastructure development, healthcare, education, affordable housing, and environmental restoration. Increased government investment would generate demand for workers across numerous industries, creating employment opportunities while stimulating local economies and strengthening the financial stability of European communities. FDI investment would also increase by 80% as investors would see Europe as a safer target to invest in if the treaty would be ratified. These social services would benefit immensely, and the average European citizen would begin to see tangible results. Poverty could decrease substantially, unemployment could fall, and European governments could make greater progress toward addressing their national debt burdens, ultimately improving the material conditions of their populations. Under this economic arrangement, the United States could also benefit significantly. Billions of dollars could flow from European governments to the United States in exchange for the continued provision of nuclear deterrence and the security guarantees associated with the transatlantic alliance. Rather than requiring every European government to independently maintain and modernize its own nuclear-related infrastructure, resources could be redistributed through a system of collective security and financial cooperation. This arrangement could create additional economic opportunities in the United States while allowing European governments to direct a greater proportion of their domestic resources toward public welfare. The resulting investment could support poverty reduction, hunger prevention, improved healthcare accessibility, expanded educational opportunities, affordable housing, infrastructure modernization, and long-term economic development. Ultimately, the central economic question is not simply how much Europe spends on nuclear capabilities, but how those resources could be allocated to improve the lives of the people those governments are responsible for protecting.

6.2 Analysis

Public investment can increase productive capacity when it targets genuine bottlenecks and is competently delivered. The IMF’s 2025 Fiscal Monitor finds that reallocating spending toward infrastructure, education, health, and research can improve long-run output, while also emphasizing efficiency and institutional quality. This supports the claim that every dollar saved from nuclear programmes generates an automatic multiplier towards benefiting social programs benefiting the European people.

7. Social investment and European living standards

Europe has substantial social infrastructure and persistent gaps. In 2025, approximately 92.7 million people in the EU—20.9% of the population—were at risk of poverty or social exclusion. The EU unemployment rate was 6.0%. These baseline figures demonstrate that poverty and labour-market problems remain policy priorities, but they do not prove that nuclear disarmament would directly solve them.

7.1 Housing

The European Commission reports that EU house prices have risen by more than 60% since 2013 and that the EU needs more than two million homes annually to meet demand, around 650,000 above the current construction rate. It estimates that the additional housing supply would require approximately €153 billion in annual investment. Housing is therefore a concrete potential destination for public and private capital, but it requires planning reform, skilled construction labour, land availability, and coordination with local authorities.

7.2 Health and education

Investment in healthcare and education can raise human capital and resilience. However, capital expenditure must be distinguished from operating expenditure: building hospitals without funding staff, supplies, and ongoing services does not create a functioning health system. Similarly, school construction must be paired with teacher recruitment, training, and sustained budgets. The report recommends a multi-year public investment framework rather than one-off spending announcements.

7.3 Employment and skills

Construction, grid modernization, healthcare, education, manufacturing, research, and environmental restoration can generate employment. The scale of net job creation depends on labour-market slack, migration, wage pressures, skills shortages, imports, and whether public spending crowds out or attracts private investment. The EIB’s investment survey identifies skills shortages, energy costs, and uncertainty among major barriers reported by European firms. A serious programme would therefore fund apprenticeships, technical education, credential recognition, and labour mobility alongside infrastructure.

8. Transatlantic cooperation

The report’s preferred arrangement is a transatlantic security compact built around conventional collective defence, intelligence, logistics, industrial cooperation, and resilience. This compact could preserve deep U.S.–European defence ties while European TPNW parties relevant nuclear missiles would be subject to ownership by NATO. What's more: commercial, industrial, and conventional defence cooperation would continue under the TPNW.

8.1 What a workable compact could include

  • Joint conventional capability planning under NATO’s Defence Planning Process.
  • Integrated air and missile defence, counter-drone systems, and early warning.
  • Stockpiles, maintenance capacity, secure logistics, and military mobility.
  • Cyber defence, space resilience, intelligence cooperation, and protection of critical infrastructure.
  • Research partnerships in semiconductors, AI, aerospace, robotics, biotechnology, and energy systems.
  • Transparent financing for conventional capabilities, without payments earmarked for prohibited nuclear activities.

9.1 Country groupings

Five states face immediate stationing issues because of U.S. nuclear weapons hosted on their territory. France and the United Kingdom face the more fundamental issue of their own nuclear arsenals. Frontline states—especially Estonia, Finland, Latvia, Lithuania, Poland, and Romania—require credible reassurance before any change to nuclear posture. Non-host states have fewer physical removal requirements but still need to assess Article 1(e), nuclear planning participation, and alliance policy.

10. Europe in 2036: a ten-year scenario audit

10.1 What would happen 10 years after Europe ratifies the treaty.

In a scenario where Europe ratified the Treaty on the Prohibition of Nuclear Weapons (TPNW), this paper will evaluate the likely effects of it. Firstly, the chance of a recession would drop significantly, and the world would be safer. In portion due to FDI investment, and the exponential nature of European gdp growth, this paper will take a conservative stance and say Europe GDP will continue to go up with the low-ball estimate of 25 trillion, over a period of 10 years, in reality it’s likely to be much higher. Over these 10 years Europe will undergo a significant economic, technological, and social transformation. Because European governments redirected substantial financial resources toward domestic investment, right away after the treaty was ratified, to public services, scientific research, technological development, and energy infrastructure the European and American economy will both be booming. This is quantified via the low estimate that over these 10 years, across Europe, unemployment will declined by approximately 70%, while poverty will decreased by 40%. These improvements reflected a significant expansion of employment opportunities, supported by government investment in infrastructure, healthcare, education, manufacturing, affordable housing, and environmental restoration. Public investment generated demand across numerous industries, creating employment opportunities while stimulating private-sector development and strengthening regional economies. Foreign direct investment increased by approximately 268%, as the continent experienced a major expansion in economic activity and technological development. Over the same decade, Europe's cumulative GDP growth reached an extraordinary additional 25 trillion dollars. This expansion substantially strengthened government revenues, improved fiscal stability, and enabled European countries to make significant progress in reducing their national debt. The resulting economic growth also expanded the resources available for public welfare, allowing governments to improve social services and raise the overall standard of living for their populations. In America, approximately 20 million jobs will have been created across numerous industries, including aerospace engineering, defense manufacturing, advanced robotics, artificial intelligence, semiconductor production, biotechnology, pharmaceutical research, medical technology, renewable energy, nuclear fusion research, electrical infrastructure, construction, transportation, logistics, telecommunications, software development, cybersecurity, scientific research, higher education, and advanced materials manufacturing. Increased transatlantic investment would stimulate American businesses, expand industrial production, strengthen domestic supply chains, and generate additional demand for skilled and unskilled labor. These employment opportunities would contribute to higher household incomes, increased consumer spending, and greater economic activity throughout the United States.

14. Conclusion

European NATO members should pursue ratification of the TPNW as a serious long-term policy objective, but the case for ratification must be built on legal accuracy, strategic realism, and verifiable economic evidence. The treaty’s humanitarian purpose is clear, and its prohibitions are explicit. The challenge is that current European security arrangements include nuclear weapons hosted on the territory of five NATO Allies, independent arsenals in France and the United Kingdom, and alliance-wide participation in nuclear planning and deterrence policy.

The report’s most important conclusion is that the relocation proposal in the first locked quotation does not resolve the territorial problem merely by placing weapons on a U.S.-operated base in Europe. A host country’s sovereignty is not automatically displaced by a foreign base agreement. The second quotation makes a compelling ethical argument about opportunity costs, but the claimed €/$2 trillion spending figure and 80% FDI increase require evidence. The third quotation offers an ambitious 2036 vision, but its unemployment, poverty, FDI, GDP, and U.S. employment numbers must be modelled as scenario assumptions rather than described as likely outcomes.

A defensible pro-ratification position is therefore conditional but substantive: coordinate European ratification; end prohibited stationing and assistance; protect NATO’s conventional collective-defence capabilities; maintain safeguards and verification; and redirect only verified savings into high-return public investment. Ratification should be the outcome of a credible transition—not a substitute for one. Europe can make a stronger case for nuclear disarmament when it demonstrates that humanitarian commitments, collective defence, and social development can be pursued through a coherent and legally compliant security architecture.

Appendix A. Core data definitions

  • TPNW party: A state that has ratified, acceded to, accepted, or approved the treaty, as recorded by the UN depositary.
  • FDI flow: Cross-border direct investment transactions over a period; not the same as FDI stock or greenfield project announcements.
  • AROPE: At risk of poverty or social exclusion, a composite EU indicator covering income poverty, severe material and social deprivation, and very low work intensity.
  • Unemployment rate: Unemployed people as a share of the labour force, not as a share of the entire population.
  • Defence expenditure: NATO/EDA accounting categories that include multiple functions beyond nuclear weapons.
  • Cumulative additional GDP: Sum of annual output above a defined counterfactual over a specified horizon; distinct from the level of GDP in the final year.
