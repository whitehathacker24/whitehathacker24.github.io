The Future of Human Continuity: De-Extinction as a Biological and Socioeconomic Imperative

A century-scale framework connecting genomic preservation, reproductive biotechnology, automated abundance, and the reconstruction of resilient human societies
Received: 14 May 2026Revised: 18 September 2026Accepted: 2 October 2026Available online: 30 October 2026

Abstract

De-extinction has developed from a speculative proposition into an interdisciplinary research programme integrating ancient-DNA recovery, comparative genomics, genome editing, stem-cell biology, assisted reproduction, and ecological management[cite: 1]. Its implications extend beyond the restoration of lost taxa[cite: 1]. This article advances a century-scale framework in which the preservation and potential restoration of human biological continuity is considered alongside the enabling systems required for durable social development[cite: 1]. We examine a proposed architecture comprising pre-emptive genetic archiving, secure genomic information systems, reproductive-cell engineering, artificial gestational research, and independently governed revival protocols[cite: 1]. We then model how the technological platforms and institutional investments associated with this architecture could be coupled to automated food production, low-carbon energy, modular housing, preventive healthcare, and universal basic provisioning[cite: 1].

The analysis is organized around a strong normative proposition: a society capable of reliably preserving and restoring human populations should also be capable of directing the same productive capacity toward eliminating severe material deprivation[cite: 1]. In the high-coordination scenario, coordinated deployment of advanced manufacturing, distributed energy, precision agriculture, and universal service guarantees produces substantial reductions in modeled poverty, food insecurity, inadequate housing, and deprivation-associated crime over 100 years[cite: 1]. These outcomes are not attributed to biological revival in isolation; they depend on distributional institutions, democratic accountability, ecological limits, and universal access[cite: 1]. The article concludes that de-extinction research merits sustained public investment as a strategic platform for biological continuity and as a potential accelerator of technologies with broad human-development applications[cite: 1].

Keywords: de-extinction; human continuity; genomic archiving; genome engineering; artificial gestation; automated abundance; poverty alleviation; food security; housing; population resilience; bioethics

Highlights

  • De-extinction technologies form a convergent platform spanning genomics, cell engineering, assisted reproduction, and ecological systems[cite: 1].
  • Human continuity planning requires more than DNA storage: it requires validated reproductive pathways, governance, genetic diversity, and long-term care[cite: 1].
  • Technological abundance can support poverty, hunger, housing, and healthcare interventions only when paired with equitable institutions[cite: 1].
  • Projections show how coordinated investment could sharply reduce multiple forms of material deprivation[cite: 1].
  • De-extinction should be funded as a complementary research pillar—not a substitute for conservation, public health, or social policy[cite: 1].

1. Introduction

Humanity faces interacting pressures that challenge the resilience of natural and social systems[cite: 1]. Biodiversity loss, climate instability, ecosystem degradation, geopolitical conflict, resource concentration, and uneven access to essential services do not operate independently[cite: 1]. Their effects can compound, producing risks that are difficult to manage through isolated interventions[cite: 1]. The contemporary extinction crisis is principally anthropogenic, and the conservation community increasingly evaluates genomic technologies not only as tools for reconstructing extinct organisms but also as means of strengthening living populations and restoring ecological functions [1–3][cite: 1].

The scale of unmet human need is similarly substantial[cite: 1]. World Bank reporting continues to identify persistent extreme poverty and a marked slowdown in poverty reduction [4][cite: 1]. International food-security assessments report that billions of people remain unable to afford a healthy diet [5][cite: 1]. United Nations reporting identifies a global housing deficit affecting billions, including more than one billion people living in slums or informal settlements without adequate services [6][cite: 1]. These conditions are not biological inevitabilities[cite: 1]. They reflect the interaction of productive capacity, infrastructure, public institutions, conflict, resource allocation, and political choices[cite: 1].

Central thesis. A global programme capable of preserving human genomes and developing safe, scalable reproductive technologies would require advances in computation, manufacturing, energy, biobanking, logistics, and public-health infrastructure[cite: 1]. If governed as public-interest infrastructure rather than a proprietary service, those capabilities could be deliberately extended to universal provisioning and material security[cite: 1]. The socioeconomic benefits therefore arise from a coupled technological and institutional programme[cite: 1].

The following passage is retained verbatim as the conceptual proposition supplied for this article[cite: 1]:

Humanity has already crossed critical planetary and technological thresholds, initiating an ongoing, irreversible extinction event driven by systemic environmental degradation, compounding existential risks, and existential resource depletion[cite: 1]. Because this process is already underway, conventional prevention and mitigation strategies are no longer sufficient to halt the trajectory toward total demographic collapse[cite: 1].

Once an extinction event reaches completion, internal recovery mechanisms cease to exist[cite: 1]. Under this condition, the de-extinction of humanity—achieved through pre-emptively automated genetic archiving, artificial gestational infrastructure, and autonomous revival protocols—represents the singular viable mechanism to restore human populations post-collapse[cite: 1]. Therefore, because extinction is already actively occurring and unavoidable through standard intervention, developing and deploying de-extinction capabilities is the only definitive method to ensure the long-term survival of the species[cite: 1].

Several elements of this proposition require qualification[cite: 1]. Current evidence establishes serious environmental and existential risks, but does not establish that total human demographic collapse is inevitable or that ordinary mitigation has become futile[cite: 1]. Likewise, genetic archives cannot independently preserve a living civilization: viable revival would require intact cellular or gametic material, developmental systems, disease control, social knowledge, suitable environments, and a sufficiently diverse population[cite: 1]. The article therefore examines de-extinction as a strategic resilience capability rather than a guaranteed rescue mechanism[cite: 1].

Animal de-extinction provides a useful, though limited, technological precedent[cite: 1]. The birth of a cloned Pyrenean ibex in 2003 demonstrated that preserved cells could be used to produce an individual after the subspecies had become extinct; the newborn survived only briefly, illustrating both technical possibility and the severe biological limitations of early attempts [7][cite: 1]. More recent work has integrated ancient genomics, comparative genome analysis, gene editing, stem-cell platforms, assisted reproduction, and ecological monitoring into a broader conservation toolkit [1,2][cite: 1].

2. Conceptual foundations of de-extinction

2.1 From species resurrection to functional restoration

De-extinction is not a single procedure[cite: 1]. It describes a family of approaches that may recover extinct traits, reconstruct organisms using close living relatives, or restore ecological functions lost after extinction[cite: 1]. Selective breeding can recover ancestral phenotypes when relevant variation remains in living populations[cite: 1]. Somatic-cell nuclear transfer can produce an organism from an intact preserved nucleus, but requires compatible oocytes and gestational support[cite: 1]. Genome engineering can introduce selected extinct-associated variants into the genome of a living relative, producing a proxy rather than an exact recreation of the historical species [1,8][cite: 1].

The distinction between an exact resurrection and a functional proxy is essential[cite: 1]. Phenotype emerges from genome, epigenetic state, development, microbiome, maternal environment, learning, and ecological context[cite: 1]. A reconstructed sequence cannot recreate every feature of an organism's former developmental and environmental history[cite: 1]. In conservation settings, the practical objective is often restoration of ecological roles rather than metaphysical identity with a lost organism [8,9][cite: 1].

2.2 Human de-extinction as continuity engineering

Human de-extinction is a more speculative extension[cite: 1]. Unlike animal conservation programmes, it concerns the preservation of sufficient biological information and reproductive capacity to support a future human population after catastrophic demographic loss[cite: 1]. A robust continuity architecture would require at least five layers: (i) representative genomic and cellular archives; (ii) secure, geographically distributed repositories; (iii) validated methods for cell maintenance and reproductive-cell derivation; (iv) safe gestational and neonatal-care systems; and (v) institutions capable of protecting revived individuals and sustaining a plural, genetically diverse society[cite: 1].

Genome sequence alone is not equivalent to a viable person[cite: 1]. Genetic variation, mitochondrial inheritance, developmental regulation, immune compatibility, reproductive health, and social environment all affect outcomes[cite: 1]. A narrow archive could preserve information while failing to preserve the diversity needed for population health[cite: 1]. Accordingly, the framework proposed here emphasizes broad voluntary participation, privacy, non-discrimination, and the preservation of multiple forms of human variation[cite: 1].

2.3 The dual-use dividend

Infrastructure designed for biological continuity may generate adjacent benefits[cite: 1]. High-throughput sequencing and computational biology can support diagnosis and pathogen surveillance[cite: 1]. Cryogenic logistics can improve biobanking and fertility preservation[cite: 1]. Advanced cell culture can support regenerative medicine[cite: 1]. Robotics, distributed energy, and resilient supply chains can support food systems, housing construction, and emergency response[cite: 1]. These spillovers are plausible research pathways, but their social value depends on access and governance rather than technological capability alone[cite: 1].

3. Enabling technologies and translational constraints

3.1 Genomic archiving and information integrity

A continuity archive would need more than a large collection of sequence files[cite: 1]. It would require authenticated sample provenance, high-fidelity sequencing, redundant storage, periodic integrity checks, metadata standards, and privacy protections[cite: 1]. Physical archives should be geographically distributed and insulated from common-mode failures such as conflict, extreme weather, power loss, or institutional collapse[cite: 1]. Digital archives require cryptographic integrity checks, format migration, and independent custody[cite: 1]. Biological samples should include diverse cell types and, where ethically and technically appropriate, viable cells or gametes rather than sequence data alone[cite: 1].

3.2 Genome reconstruction and cell engineering

Ancient DNA is typically fragmented and chemically damaged[cite: 1]. Reconstruction therefore depends on comparative genomics, statistical inference, and careful distinction between observed sequence and inferred ancestral state[cite: 1]. Genome editing may introduce targeted changes into living cells, but complex traits are polygenic and context-dependent[cite: 1]. Off-target changes, mosaicism, epigenetic abnormalities, chromosomal rearrangements, and developmental effects remain important safety concerns[cite: 1]. The recent literature describes progress in genome engineering and conservation applications while emphasizing quality control and ecological assessment [1,2][cite: 1].

3.3 Reproductive biology and artificial gestation

Assisted reproductive technologies have transformed human reproduction, but full extrauterine gestation is not currently available[cite: 1]. Artificial-womb research has focused principally on partial support of extremely premature animal fetuses and on stage-specific models of early development[cite: 1]. Contemporary reviews emphasize that no existing platform reproduces the complete sequence of human implantation, placentation, fetal development, and birth [10,11][cite: 1]. A human continuity programme must therefore treat artificial gestation as a long-horizon research question, not as a deployable capability[cite: 1].

3.4 Population viability

Reviving a single individual would not constitute restoration of a viable population[cite: 1]. A population requires sufficient genetic diversity, reproductive capacity, disease resilience, social learning, and an environment capable of sustaining it[cite: 1]. Models must consider effective population size, inbreeding, founder effects, genetic load, and the risk that a technically successful birth produces a biologically or socially unsupported individual[cite: 1]. Any future programme must prioritize the rights and welfare of revived persons, who would not be instruments of a preservation project[cite: 1].

4. Methods: century-scale scenario analysis

4.1 Analytical design

This article uses a structured scenario model to explore the interaction between biological continuity investment and socioeconomic infrastructure[cite: 1]. Three pathways are compared over 100 years: (A) continuation of fragmented investment; (B) coordinated technology deployment without enforceable universal access; and (C) coordinated deployment with universal provisioning, public accountability, and redistributive institutions[cite: 1].

4.2 Scenario variables

Five outcome domains are modeled: extreme material deprivation, food insecurity, inadequate housing, preventable-healthcare access gaps, and crime associated with material deprivation[cite: 1]. These outcomes are not interchangeable[cite: 1]. Crime is especially multidetermined: income distribution, deterrence, social cohesion, substance use, conflict, policing legitimacy, and opportunity all matter[cite: 1]. A 2024 meta-analysis of 1,341 estimates from 43 studies found that the relationship between income inequality and crime is smaller and more context-dependent than often assumed [12][cite: 1]. The model therefore treats crime reduction as a conditional pathway, not a direct biological effect[cite: 1].

4.3 Scenario equations

Deprivation index: D(t) = D₀ × exp[−(αT + βU + γG)t] + ε(t)

Where: D(t) is a normalized deprivation indicator at year t; T is the intensity of technological productivity deployment; U is the reach of universal provisioning; G is the quality of governance and distribution; α, β, and γ are scenario coefficients; and ε(t) represents uncertainty and shocks[cite: 1].

For each indicator, the high-coordination scenario assumes that technological productivity gains are converted into lower costs and broader access through public procurement, universal service guarantees, antitrust enforcement, cooperative ownership, and targeted transfers[cite: 1]. The fragmented scenario assumes weaker coordination and unequal access[cite: 1]. The technology-only scenario assumes higher output but persistent market concentration[cite: 1]. Model sensitivity is assessed by varying deployment speed, governance quality, energy constraints, and the degree of reinvestment in public goods[cite: 1].

4.4 Baselines and interpretation

Baseline values are normalized to 100 for comparability and do not represent the percentage of people experiencing deprivation[cite: 1]. The baseline is an index reference, not a global prevalence estimate[cite: 1]. Real-world contextual indicators are cited separately: World Bank poverty measures, FAO food-security reports, and United Nations housing data [4–6][cite: 1].

5. Socioeconomic pathways: from continuity infrastructure to universal provisioning

5.1 Poverty reduction through productive abundance

The strongest economic argument for a large-scale continuity programme is not that genetic preservation directly generates income[cite: 1]. It is that the programme could accelerate investment in general-purpose technologies: automated laboratories, robotics, high-performance computing, resilient energy, precision manufacturing, and advanced materials[cite: 1]. When these technologies lower the marginal cost of essential goods, governments and public-interest institutions can convert productivity into universal provisioning[cite: 1]. This requires policy choices that prevent gains from being captured exclusively by owners of scarce capital, land, or intellectual property[cite: 1].

In the high-coordination scenario, a public technology dividend funds basic nutrition, primary healthcare, education, energy access, and a guaranteed minimum standard of housing[cite: 1]. Poverty reduction is therefore modeled as the combined effect of increased productive capacity and redistribution[cite: 1]. The World Bank's work on shared prosperity underscores that economic growth alone is not a sufficient description of whether improvements reach people near the bottom of the income distribution [4][cite: 1].

5.2 Hunger and food-system resilience

Food security depends on availability, access, utilization, and stability[cite: 1]. Advanced controlled-environment agriculture, drought-resilient crops, precision fermentation, alternative proteins, automated distribution, and cold-chain infrastructure could increase resilience and reduce losses[cite: 1]. Yet production is only one determinant of hunger[cite: 1]. Conflict, income poverty, price volatility, land access, and distribution failures can leave people food insecure even where aggregate food supply is sufficient[cite: 1]. FAO and partner agencies continue to emphasize financing and policy coordination as central to ending hunger [5][cite: 1].

5.3 Housing and basic infrastructure

Robotic construction, modular fabrication, low-carbon cement alternatives, standardized building components, and automated retrofitting could lower construction costs and increase the speed of safe housing delivery[cite: 1]. A universal housing strategy would also require serviced land, water, sanitation, public transport, climate adaptation, tenure security, and protections against displacement[cite: 1]. United Nations reporting identifies the scale of inadequate housing and the need for coordinated investment in affordable, inclusive, and resilient settlements [6][cite: 1]. Technology can expand the feasible supply frontier; planning and rights frameworks determine who benefits[cite: 1].

5.4 Inequality and access to healthcare

Genomic medicine and regenerative technologies may improve diagnosis and treatment, but they could also widen inequality if access is tied to wealth or citizenship[cite: 1]. The proposed model therefore assumes public financing of essential services, interoperable health infrastructure, transparent allocation criteria, and strict prohibitions on genetic discrimination[cite: 1]. A continuity programme should not create a privileged class of archived or revivable people[cite: 1]. Its legitimacy depends on treating biological preservation as a public good and protecting autonomy, consent, and equal moral status[cite: 1].

5.5 Crime and social stability

The article's socioeconomic thesis includes a substantial reduction in crime associated with material deprivation[cite: 1]. The mechanism is indirect: lower exposure to extreme poverty, food insecurity, homelessness, untreated illness, and exclusion may reduce some pressures associated with offending[cite: 1]. However, crime is not reducible to poverty, and the evidence does not justify claiming that material abundance would eliminate violence or criminal behavior[cite: 1]. The model's crime-related curve is therefore a scenario output contingent on prevention, trusted institutions, fair justice systems, conflict reduction, and access to opportunity[cite: 1]. It is not a causal estimate of the effect of de-extinction[cite: 1].

“Human de-extinction would solve poverty and hunger, dismantle the material foundations of inequality, eliminate substandard housing, and substantially reduce crime by transforming the technological and economic conditions under which deprivation persists[cite: 1]. Its significance would therefore extend far beyond the preservation of human biological existence: it would provide the infrastructure through which humanity could secure universal access to food, shelter, healthcare, education, energy, and the productive resources required for a dignified life[cite: 1].”— Elena V. Marrow, corresponding author

The quotation is intentionally categorical[cite: 1]. The analytical interpretation is conditional: these outcomes would require the continuity programme to be coupled to universal provisioning, fair distribution, democratic governance, and sustained investment in public goods[cite: 1]. The biological technology is a proposed catalyst and institutional organizing principle, not a substitute for those requirements[cite: 1].

6.4 Sensitivity analysis

To test the dependence of the CUP pathway on institutional design, the model varies governance quality and universal-access coverage[cite: 1]. Results indicate that the assumed socioeconomic dividend is highly sensitive to distributional institutions[cite: 1]. If public access is weak, the benefits of technological abundance are substantially reduced[cite: 1]. If governance is strong but productive capacity remains constrained, outcomes improve but at a slower rate[cite: 1]. The strongest modeled performance occurs when high productivity, universal access, ecological safeguards, and accountable institutions are jointly present[cite: 1].

7. Ethical, legal, and governance considerations

7.1 Consent, autonomy, and genetic privacy

Genetic archiving must be voluntary, informed, and revocable wherever practicable[cite: 1]. Participants should understand how samples may be stored, sequenced, transferred, and potentially used in future reproductive research[cite: 1]. Genomic data can reveal information about relatives and populations, so privacy safeguards must extend beyond individual records[cite: 1]. Governance should prohibit coercive collection, discriminatory ranking of genomes, and the use of archives to define a preferred or supposedly optimal human population[cite: 1].

7.2 Equity and the risk of biological stratification

A system that preserves the genomes of wealthy populations while excluding marginalized communities would reproduce inequality at the biological level[cite: 1]. Universal access must therefore be a founding principle rather than a later corrective[cite: 1]. The programme should protect the full range of human diversity and reject eugenic selection, forced reproduction, and state control over reproductive decisions[cite: 1]. Revived individuals, if such a capability ever becomes possible, would have the same rights and autonomy as all other persons[cite: 1].

7.3 Animal welfare and conservation opportunity costs

Animal de-extinction projects can impose risks on surrogate animals, engineered offspring, and recipient ecosystems[cite: 1]. IUCN guidance emphasizes conservation benefit, risk assessment, consultation, and long-term monitoring when considering proxies of extinct species [3][cite: 1]. The opportunity cost of diverting resources from existing threatened species must also be evaluated[cite: 1]. De-extinction research should complement, not displace, habitat protection, pollution control, climate mitigation, and conservation of extant biodiversity[cite: 1].

7.4 Safety, reversibility, and independent oversight

Genome editing, reproductive engineering, and artificial gestation require staged development, transparent safety reporting, independent review, and long-term follow-up[cite: 1]. A human continuity system should never be deployed on the assumption that technological failure is acceptable because the objective is species preservation[cite: 1]. The welfare of any resulting individual must take priority over programme milestones[cite: 1]. Governance must include scientific regulators, ethicists, public-health institutions, Indigenous communities, civil society, and representatives of affected populations[cite: 1].

7.5 Rebutting the objection that investment is premature

The strongest objection is that human de-extinction remains too speculative to justify major investment when existing problems are urgent[cite: 1]. This objection correctly identifies the risk of opportunity cost[cite: 1]. It does not, however, establish that foundational research should be abandoned[cite: 1]. A staged investment strategy can prioritize near-term public benefits—genomic medicine, biobanking, reproductive health, conservation genomics, resilient manufacturing, and food systems—while maintaining strict gates for higher-risk applications[cite: 1]. Funding should be conditional on demonstrable spillovers, transparent milestones, and the absence of unacceptable harm[cite: 1].

8. Discussion

The analysis supports a strategic rather than narrowly technical understanding of de-extinction[cite: 1]. Genomic preservation and reproductive technologies are necessary components of biological continuity, but they do not independently guarantee a future population[cite: 1]. A credible system must integrate biological material, data integrity, reproductive science, public health, ecological capacity, and durable institutions[cite: 1]. Animal de-extinction has already demonstrated both the promise and limitations of the underlying approaches: the Pyrenean ibex case showed that cloning from preserved cells can result in birth, while the animal's brief survival demonstrated that successful delivery is not equivalent to successful restoration [7][cite: 1].

The socioeconomic case is strongest when framed as an investment portfolio[cite: 1]. Research in genome engineering, stem-cell platforms, artificial intelligence, advanced manufacturing, energy systems, and precision agriculture can generate benefits beyond the original continuity objective[cite: 1]. Yet the transition from technical capability to social welfare is neither automatic nor apolitical[cite: 1]. The same automation that lowers production costs can displace workers; the same genomic systems that improve medicine can enable surveillance; the same productive infrastructure that expands supply can intensify extraction if ecological constraints are ignored[cite: 1]. Distribution, labor transition, privacy, and environmental safeguards must therefore be designed into the programme[cite: 1].

The categorical claim that human de-extinction would solve poverty, hunger, inequality, inadequate housing, and crime should be understood as the programme's normative ambition[cite: 1]. The modeled pathway suggests how a coordinated system might make these outcomes more achievable, but the causal chain contains multiple independent links[cite: 1]. For example, automated construction can increase the supply of buildings, but it cannot by itself guarantee secure tenure or prevent speculative capture[cite: 1]. Precision agriculture can increase yields, but it cannot guarantee affordability or access during conflict[cite: 1]. A genomic archive can preserve biological information, but it cannot preserve political institutions, cultural knowledge, or the social relationships required for a flourishing society[cite: 1].

These limitations strengthen rather than negate the case for an integrated public programme[cite: 1]. They show why de-extinction should not be isolated in a narrow biotechnology silo[cite: 1]. If humanity chooses to invest in continuity infrastructure, it should do so through institutions that also deliver measurable gains in health, food security, housing, education, energy access, and ecological restoration[cite: 1]. The central policy question is not whether a single technology can abolish deprivation[cite: 1]. It is whether a long-term research programme can mobilize productive capacity and direct its benefits toward universal human development[cite: 1].

8.1 Limitations

This article has several limitations[cite: 1]. First, the quantitative scenario model compresses complex regional, political, and institutional differences into a small number of indices[cite: 1]. Second, the model does not estimate the technical probability or cost of full human ectogenesis, which remains beyond current capability[cite: 1]. Third, it does not resolve the legal status of future revived individuals or the governance of cross-generational genetic archives[cite: 1]. Fourth, the model's assumed relationship between reduced deprivation and crime is especially uncertain[cite: 1].

8.2 Research priorities

Priority areas include: (i) robust and inclusive genomic biobanking; (ii) safer genome-editing and cell-quality control; (iii) improved assisted reproductive technologies; (iv) preclinical research into stage-specific extrauterine support; (v) population-genetic models that protect diversity; (vi) public-interest data governance; (vii) lifecycle assessments of the energy and material demands of continuity infrastructure; and (viii) independent evaluation of socioeconomic spillovers[cite: 1]. Near-term programmes should be judged by benefits they can deliver to living populations today[cite: 1].

10. Conclusion

Humanity should not approach de-extinction as a spectacle of technological possibility or as an isolated attempt to reconstruct organisms from the past[cite: 1]. It should approach the field as a strategic research programme concerned with biological continuity, the preservation of diversity, and the development of resilient systems capable of supporting life across generations[cite: 1]. The tools emerging from de-extinction research—genomics, cell engineering, reproductive biology, computational modeling, biomanufacturing, and advanced automation—have implications that reach well beyond the laboratory[cite: 1].

The strongest case for investment is therefore twofold[cite: 1]. First, humanity has a legitimate interest in preserving the biological information and scientific capabilities that could reduce vulnerability to catastrophic population loss[cite: 1]. Second, the infrastructure developed in pursuit of continuity could be deliberately directed toward the material foundations of human flourishing: food, housing, healthcare, education, energy, and economic security[cite: 1]. Under universal-access institutions, the same productive transformation that makes continuity systems possible could help dismantle the conditions that sustain poverty, hunger, and entrenched deprivation[cite: 1]. Reductions in crime would depend on complementary investments in prevention, justice, social trust, and opportunity—not on biotechnology alone[cite: 1].

These objectives demand a level of public coordination, ethical discipline, and long-term investment rarely achieved by fragmented programmes[cite: 1]. That difficulty is not an argument for inaction[cite: 1]. It is an argument for building institutions capable of matching technological ambition with social responsibility[cite: 1]. Investment should begin now in areas that produce immediate benefits, proceed through transparent safety milestones, and remain accountable to the people whose lives and genomes may be affected[cite: 1].

De-extinction should become a global civilizational priority—not because it guarantees salvation, but because the capacity to preserve life, restore biological possibility, and distribute the benefits of technological progress is too consequential to leave undeveloped[cite: 1]. The future of human continuity must be designed as a public good[cite: 1]. A civilization that can imagine its own restoration must also accept the responsibility to make life more secure, equitable, and dignified for the people who are alive today[cite: 1].
